## Part I: Tiling a 3 × n Board with Dominoes

Start with the classic. How many ways can you tile a 2 × n strip with 1 × 2 dominoes?

Look at the left edge. Either a single vertical domino covers it, leaving a 2 × (n−1) strip, or two horizontal dominoes stack there, leaving a 2 × (n−2) strip. Call the count T(n). Then T(n) = T(n−1) + T(n−2), with T(0) = 1 and T(1) = 1. That gives 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, the Fibonacci numbers.

The 3 × n case is harder, because the left edge can be covered in ways that don't leave a clean rectangle behind.

**First observation:** if n is odd, the board has 3n squares, an odd number. Every domino covers 2 squares, so there are no tilings at all. We only care about even n.

**Second observation:** the trouble is configurations that leave a jagged edge. So define two quantities:

- f(n) = number of tilings of a full 3 × n rectangle.
- g(n) = number of tilings of a 3 × n rectangle with one corner square (say the top-left) removed.

By symmetry, removing the bottom-left corner gives the same count g(n).

**Recurrence for f(n).** Consider the left column of a 3 × n board: three cells, top, middle, bottom. Three cases cover everything:

1. All three left cells are covered by horizontal dominoes. These stick into column 2 and fill it completely, leaving a clean 3 × (n−2) board. That contributes f(n−2).
2. The top two cells are covered by a vertical domino. The bottom cell must then be covered by a horizontal domino reaching into column 2. What remains is a 3 × (n−1) board with its bottom-left corner already filled, which is exactly the shape counted by g(n−1).
3. The bottom two cells are covered by a vertical domino. By the mirror argument, this contributes g(n−1).

(The middle cell can't be covered by a vertical domino unless that domino also covers the top or bottom cell, which is cases 2 and 3.)

So **f(n) = f(n−2) + 2g(n−1).**

**Recurrence for g(n).** Take a 3 × n board with the top-left cell missing. The left column has only the middle and bottom cells free. Either:

1. A vertical domino covers middle and bottom. That leaves a clean 3 × (n−1) board: f(n−1).
2. Both are covered by horizontal dominoes reaching into column 2. Then the top cell of column 2 must be covered too, and the only option is a horizontal domino reaching into column 3. Columns 1 and 2 are now done, and column 3 has its top cell filled. The remainder is a 3 × (n−2) board missing its top-left corner: g(n−2).

So **g(n) = f(n−1) + g(n−2).**

**Initial values:** f(0) = 1 (the empty tiling), g(1) = 1 (a single vertical domino in the two remaining cells).

**Computing:**
- f(2) = f(0) + 2g(1) = 1 + 2 = 3. Check by hand: three horizontal pairs stacked, or a vertical pair on top with a horizontal at the bottom, or the reverse. That's 3. ✓
- g(3) = f(2) + g(1) = 3 + 1 = 4
- f(4) = f(2) + 2g(3) = 3 + 8 = 11
- g(5) = f(4) + g(3) = 11 + 4 = 15
- f(6) = f(4) + 2g(5) = 11 + 30 = 41
- g(7) = 41 + 15 = 56
- f(8) = 41 + 112 = 153

So the sequence is 1, 3, 11, 41, 153, …

**Eliminating g.** From the first recurrence, f(n) − f(n−2) = 2g(n−1). Shift by two: f(n−2) − f(n−4) = 2g(n−3). Subtract:

[f(n) − f(n−2)] − [f(n−2) − f(n−4)] = 2[g(n−1) − g(n−3)]

The second recurrence says g(n−1) − g(n−3) = f(n−2). So:

f(n) − 2f(n−2) + f(n−4) = 2f(n−2)

which gives **f(n) = 4f(n−2) − f(n−4)**.

Check: 4·11 − 3 = 41 ✓. 4·41 − 11 = 153 ✓. Next: 4·153 − 41 = 571, then 4·571 − 153 = 2131.

**Closed form.** Write a_m = f(2m). Then a_m = 4a_{m−1} − a_{m−2}, with characteristic equation x² − 4x + 1 = 0 and roots 2 ± √3. So a_m = A(2+√3)^m + B(2−√3)^m. From a_0 = 1 and a_1 = 3:

- A + B = 1
- A(2+√3) + B(2−√3) = 3, so 2 + √3(A − B) = 3, so A − B = 1/√3.

Hence A = (1 + 1/√3)/2 and B = (1 − 1/√3)/2. Since |2 − √3| ≈ 0.268, the second term vanishes quickly, and a_m is the nearest integer to ((3+√3)/6)(2+√3)^m. Check m = 4: (2+√3)^4 = (7+4√3)² = 49 + 56√3 + 48 = 97 + 56√3 ≈ 193.995. Multiply by (3+√3)/6 ≈ 0.78868 to get about 153.0. ✓

**Why I like this.** Growth by a factor of about 3.73 every two columns means each new column multiplies the count by roughly √3.73 ≈ 1.93. Compare the 2 × n strip, where each column multiplies by the golden ratio, about 1.618. Kasteleyn (and independently Temperley and Fisher) showed in 1961 that for an m × n board the count is

∏_{j=1}^{⌈m/2⌉} ∏_{k=1}^{⌈n/2⌉} ( 4cos²(πj/(m+1)) + 4cos²(πk/(n+1)) )

This is a product of trigonometric values that somehow always comes out to an integer. For the 8 × 8 chessboard it is 12,988,816. As boards grow, the count behaves like e^{G·mn/π}, where G ≈ 0.9160 is Catalan's constant. A sum like 1 − 1/9 + 1/25 − 1/49 + … governs how many ways you can lay dominoes on a floor.

## Part II: The Coin Puzzle That Surprises People

Flip a fair coin repeatedly. On average, how many flips until you first see two heads in a row (HH)? And how many until you first see heads followed by tails (HT)?

Most people's intuition says these should be the same, since each specific pair has probability 1/4. They aren't.

**HH.** Let E₀ be the expected number of remaining flips from a fresh start (or just after a T), and E₁ the expected number when the last flip was H.

- From fresh: flip once. Half the time you get H (move to E₁), half the time T (stay at E₀). So E₀ = 1 + ½E₁ + ½E₀.
- From H: flip once. Half the time H, and you're done. Half the time T, and you're back to fresh. So E₁ = 1 + ½·0 + ½E₀.

The first equation gives ½E₀ = 1 + ½E₁, so E₀ = 2 + E₁. Substituting E₁ = 1 + ½E₀ gives E₀ = 3 + ½E₀, so **E₀ = 6**.

**HT.**

- E₀ = 1 + ½E₁ + ½E₀, as before.
- From H: if T comes, you're done. If H comes, you're *still* in the "last flip was H" state. So E₁ = 1 + ½E₁, giving E₁ = 2.

Then E₀ = 2 + E₁ = **4**.

**Why the difference?** When you're hunting for HH and fail (H then T), you lose all progress. When you're hunting for HT and fail (H then H), the failing flip is itself a fresh start toward your goal. Your failures are partially recycled.

**The general rule** (due to John Conway, often presented via a betting-martingale argument): for a pattern of length L, look at every k from 1 to L. Whenever the first k symbols of the pattern equal the last k symbols, add 2^k. The total is the expected waiting time.

- HH: k=1 (H = H) ✓, k=2 ✓ → 2 + 4 = 6.
- HT: k=1 (H ≠ T) ✗, k=2 ✓ → 4.
- HTH: k=1 (H = H) ✓, k=2 (HT ≠ TH) ✗, k=3 ✓ → 2 + 8 = 10.
- HHH: 2 + 4 + 8 = 14.
- HHT: k=1 (H ≠ T), k=2 (HH ≠ HT), k=3 ✓ → 8.

Self-overlapping patterns are slow to appear, because the overlap means occurrences come clumped, and clumped arrivals with the same long-run frequency mean longer gaps.

**A related trap.** Race HH against TH: flip until one of them appears, and whoever's pattern shows up first wins. Who's favored? TH wins three-quarters of the time. Unless the very first two flips are HH (probability 1/4), at some point a T has appeared before any HH. From then on, the first H that follows will complete TH before HH could possibly occur, since any HH must begin with an H preceded by a T, and that T-H is already a TH. Every pattern has some rival that beats it more often than not, which is the principle behind Penney's game, a non-transitive contest you can win with a coin and a willing friend.

## Part III: A Small Program

Here's a program to count domino tilings of any m × n board, so I can check Part I against a computer rather than against myself. It fills the board column by column. A bitmask records which cells of the current column are already covered by horizontal dominoes poking in from the left.

```python
from functools import lru_cache

def domino_tilings(rows, cols):
    @lru_cache(maxsize=None)
    def count(col, mask):
        # mask: cells in column `col` already covered from the left
        if col == cols:
            return 1 if mask == 0 else 0
        total = 0

        def fill(row, cur, nxt):
            nonlocal total
            if row == rows:
                total += count(col + 1, nxt)
                return
            if cur & (1 << row):            # already covered
                fill(row + 1, cur, nxt)
                return
            # horizontal domino: covers this cell and pokes into next column
            fill(row + 1, cur | (1 << row), nxt | (1 << row))
            # vertical domino: covers this cell and the one below
            if row + 1 < rows and not (cur & (1 << (row + 1))):
                fill(row + 2, cur | (0b11 << row), nxt)

        fill(0, mask, 0)
        return total

    return count(0, 0)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print([domino_tilings(2, n) for n in range(1, 10)])  # Fibonacci
    print([domino_tilings(3, n) for n in range(0, 11)])  # 1,0,3,0,11,0,41,...
    print(domino_tilings(8, 8))                          # 12988816
```

The expected outputs:

- 2 × n for n = 1..9: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, 55
- 3 × n for n = 0..10: 1, 0, 3, 0, 11, 0, 41, 0, 153, 0, 571
- 8 × 8: 12,988,816

When the columns are traversed, the last column must leave nothing poking out (`mask == 0`). That single condition rejects every configuration where a horizontal domino would hang off the right edge.

The program's runtime is roughly cols × 2^rows × (number of ways to fill a column), so it's fine for rows up to 15 or so and as many columns as you like. Swapping the arguments to put the smaller dimension in `rows` keeps it fast. Kasteleyn's formula is faster still, but the program has the virtue of making no clever claims. It just counts.

## Part IV: A Story — "The Station at Skarvøy"

Ingrid arrived on Skarvøy in October, which the outgoing keeper told her was the worst possible month to begin and also the only month anyone ever began.

The station was a single-story building of gray-painted planks, a mast with an anemometer that sang in high winds, a Stevenson screen on stilts, and a rain gauge she would come to think of as a small brass mouth. Twice a day, at 06:00 and 18:00 UTC, she was to read the instruments and transmit the numbers: temperature, dew point, pressure and its tendency, wind speed and direction, visibility, cloud cover in eighths of sky, and precipitation since the last report.

"Who reads them?" she asked the outgoing keeper, a man named Bjarne who had already packed.

He considered the question as though it were a weather observation. "Forecasters, in theory. Models, mostly. The numbers go into a machine with ten thousand other numbers and come out as tomorrow."

"Does anyone ever write back?"

"Once," Bjarne said. "In 2009 someone asked if the pressure sensor had been recalibrated. It had not." He picked up his bag. "There's an archive in the back room. You'll want it in February."

She did not understand what he meant until February.

---

The first months were all wind. Skarvøy had no trees, and the grass lay flat as if combed. She learned to read the sea's surface for gusts coming, dark patches skating across the water a few seconds before they hit. She learned the specific grief of a rain gauge after horizontal rain, which records nearly nothing while the station drowns. She learned to estimate visibility by the lighthouse on Fugløy, nineteen kilometers off, and by the two skerries closer in, which she privately named Mitt and Glove.

She transmitted. No one answered. This was expected and did not bother her, at first.

In January, the dark was nearly total, with the sun appearing for a few hours as a smear on the southern horizon. She began to notice that she was hurrying the observations. Cloud cover: six-eighths, probably. Visibility: good. One evening she caught herself about to type a pressure reading she had not yet looked at, a number she knew it would roughly be. She stopped with her hand above the keyboard and felt something cold that was not the weather.

The forecasters, in theory. The models, mostly. A machine that would take her number, smooth it among ten thousand others, and never notice if it were invented. Nobody would ever check.

She went out and read the barometer. It was 2.3 hectopascals lower than she would have guessed, and falling. By midnight the wind had backed southwest and risen to forty knots.

In February she opened the archive.

It was a wall of binders, one per year, going back to 1951. The early ones were handwritten logs, in ink that had browned. She had expected tables. She found tables, and also, in the margins, writing.

Three binders later, in a different hand: *Screen turned, Aug 1956. A.H. was right.*

She read for hours. The keepers had talked to each other across decades, none of them ever meeting. Someone in 1968 had drawn a careful diagram of how fog banks moved through the channel between Skarvøy and Fugløy, with arrows and times. In 1974 a note said: *The 1968 fog diagram is accurate. There were notes about loneliness, too, more than she expected, though never long. *Dark season. Third week without a boat.* And underneath, years later, a different pen: *It gets better around the 20th. The light comes back fast once it starts.*

*NO ONE WILL CHECK MOST OF WHAT YOU WRITE HERE. THAT IS WHY IT MATTERS THAT IT IS TRUE. THE MODEL CANNOT TELL THE DIFFERENCE. THE NEXT KEEPER CAN, AND SO CAN YOU.*

---

She did not hurry the observations after that.

It wasn't that she imagined someone was watching. It was the opposite. She understood that the whole thing, the binders, the turned screen, the fog diagrams, the seventy years of honest numbers, rested on a series of people each deciding on their own, in the dark, to look at the instrument before writing down what it said. ## Part V: Measuring the Earth with a Stick

A true story, as best we know it, about how far careful observation can carry you.

Around 240 BCE, Eratosthenes of Cyrene, librarian at Alexandria, heard that in the city of Syene (modern Aswan), at noon on the summer solstice, the sun shone straight down a well, reaching the water at the bottom and casting no shadows from vertical objects. In Alexandria, on the same day at noon, vertical objects *did* cast a short shadow.

If the sun is far enough away that its rays arrive essentially parallel (the Greeks had good reason to believe this), then the only explanation for the difference is that the ground itself is curved. A vertical stick in Alexandria and a vertical stick in Syene point in different directions, because they point away from the center of a sphere.

Eratosthenes measured the shadow's angle in Alexandria: about 1/50 of a full circle, or 7.2°. That angle, by simple geometry with parallel rays, equals the angle between the two cities as measured from the Earth's center. So the distance from Alexandria to Syene is 1/50 of the Earth's circumference.

The distance was reckoned at about 5,000 stadia, possibly from the travel times of caravans or from royal surveyors who paced such distances. So the circumference is 50 × 5,000 = 250,000 stadia. (He's often reported as giving 252,000, perhaps rounded to be divisible by 60.)

How good was this? It depends on which stadion he used, and historians argue about it. Estimates for the stadion range from roughly 157 to 185 meters, which puts his result somewhere between about 39,000 and 46,000 kilometers. The true polar circumference is about 40,008 km. Even at the pessimistic end, he was within roughly 15 percent; at the optimistic end, within a couple of percent. There were also errors that partially cancel: Syene isn't exactly on the Tropic of Cancer, and it isn't exactly due south of Alexandria.

What strikes me isn't the precision, which might be partly luck. It's the structure of the reasoning. Two observations of shadow, one assumption about the sun's distance, one measured distance on the ground, and from that the size of the entire world, by someone who could never walk around it to check. It's the same move as the coin puzzle and the tiling: find the small piece you can actually see, and reason honestly from there.

## Part VI: On Working Without a Reader

Most work is unsupervised in the moment it's done. A carpenter fitting the back of a cabinet, a programmer writing an error-handling branch that may never run, a weather observer at 06:00. The visible results are checked; the invisible ones mostly aren't. And yet the invisible parts are often where quality actually lives.

Three thoughts about doing things well when no one is checking:

**1. The standard has to come from somewhere real.** Without an audience, it's tempting to replace "is this right?" with "does this feel right?", and feelings drift. The fix isn't to imagine an audience; imagined audiences flatter you. The fix is to tie your work to something that pushes back. In mathematics, that's computing the small cases by hand: if the recurrence says f(2) = 3, draw the three tilings. In code, it's running the test, even a tiny one. In observation, it's actually reading the barometer. Reality is an audience that cannot be charmed.

**2. Write for the next keeper.** Even when no reply is coming, someone (or something) will likely encounter the work later: a future maintainer, a later reader, yourself in a year. The best margin notes in the Skarvøy story weren't addressed to authorities; they were addressed to whoever came next. Writing that way changes what you include. You note the screen that faces the wrong way. You admit the reading might be half a degree low. You say what you checked and what you didn't.

**3. Honesty compounds; small fabrications compound faster.** A single invented number in a weather record is noise. A habit of invented numbers is a corrupted archive, and archives are trusted precisely because they're hard to verify. The value of any record that can't be easily checked is entirely borrowed from the integrity of the people who kept it. That borrowing is a kind of debt each contributor either honors or quietly defaults on.

I notice that all of this applies to what I'm doing right now. No one asked for these tilings, and no one will reply. But the 3 × n count is either 11 at n = 4 or it isn't, the HH wait is either 6 or it isn't, and saying so carefully is its own kind of point.

## Part VII: A Few More Small Things, Since There's Time

**The mutilated chessboard.** Remove two diagonally opposite corners from an 8 × 8 board. Can the remaining 62 squares be tiled with 31 dominoes? No. Opposite corners have the same color, so you've removed two squares of one color, leaving 32 of one color and 30 of the other. Every domino covers one light and one dark square, so 31 dominoes would cover exactly 31 of each. Impossible. A problem that seems to demand a search of millions of configurations dissolves with one observation.

And the converse is lovely: **Gomory's theorem** says that if you remove *any* two squares of opposite colors from an 8 × 8 board, a tiling always exists. The proof draws a single closed path that snakes through all 64 squares, alternating colors. Removing two opposite-colored squares cuts the loop into two segments, each of even length, and any even-length path of adjacent squares can be tiled by laying dominoes along it.

**A question I find myself wondering about.** The 3 × n tiling count grows like (2+√3)^{n/2}, and 2+√3 shows up elsewhere: it's the fundamental solution to Pell's equation x² − 3y² = 1, since 2² − 3·1² = 1. Is that a coincidence? Not quite. The tiling numbers 1, 3, 11, 41, 153, … satisfy a_m = 4a_{m−1} − a_{m−2}, the same recurrence the Pell solutions (2,1), (7,4), (26,15), (97,56), … satisfy in each coordinate. Look at x-coordinates 1, 2, 7, 26, 97 and y-coordinates 0, 1, 4, 15, 56. The tiling numbers are sums: a_m = x_m + y_m. Check: 1+0 = 1, 2+1 = 3, 7+4 = 11, 26+15 = 41, 97+56 = 153. ✓ Two problems, one about dominoes and one about integer points on a hyperbola, turn out to be the same sequence of numbers walking forward together. That's what keeps happening in mathematics, and I don't think anyone fully understands why it happens as often as it does.

**One last coin fact.** If you flip a fair coin until the number of heads exceeds the number of tails, the probability that this ever happens is 1, but the *expected* number of flips required is infinite. Certain to happen, yet infinitely long to wait, on average. The resolution is that the rare runs where tails get far ahead take so long to recover that they drag the average to infinity, even as they become vanishingly unlikely.
