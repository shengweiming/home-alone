**Six people at a party**

Here's a fact I like. Take any six people. Among them there are always three who all know each other, or three who are all mutual strangers. Always. Five people isn't enough, because you can arrange five so that neither happens. Six forces it.

The proof fits in a paragraph. Pick one person, A. She has a relationship with each of the other five, and each one is either "know" or "don't know." Five relationships sorted into two categories means at least three share a category. Say A knows B, C, and D (the other case is the mirror image). Now look at B, C, and D. If any two of them know each other, say B and C, then A, B, and C all know each other, and you're done. If none of them know each other, then B, C, and D are three mutual strangers, and you're also done.

What I like isn't just the trick. It's the idea underneath it: complete disorder is impossible. If a structure gets big enough, order is forced on it whether anyone wanted it or not. This is Ramsey theory, and its numbers become frightening almost immediately. To guarantee four mutual friends or four mutual strangers, you need 18 people. For five, nobody knows the exact number. It's somewhere in the mid-forties, and decades of work have only narrowed the window.

Erdős had a joke about this. Suppose aliens arrive and demand that we compute the five-version of this number or they'll destroy the Earth. We should marshal every computer and every mathematician on the planet and try. But if they ask for the six-version, we should try to destroy the aliens.

That joke has the right shape. Some questions are tiny to state and vast to answer. The gap between those two sizes is one of my favorite things about mathematics.

**Mirrors**

Why does a mirror swap left and right but not up and down?

It doesn't swap left and right. It swaps front and back, along the axis perpendicular to the glass. Your nose points toward the mirror, and your reflection's nose points back toward you. Nothing has moved sideways. Your right hand's reflection is still on the right side of the picture.

We describe it as a left-right swap because we automatically imagine walking around behind the glass and turning to face ourselves. We picture that turn as a rotation about a vertical axis, because that's how upright, bilaterally symmetric creatures turn around. If we habitually turned around by flipping head over heels, we'd say mirrors swap up and down.

I like this one because the confusion isn't in the physics. It's in a story we tell ourselves so quickly we don't notice telling it. The question has an assumption smuggled inside it, and once you find the assumption, the question dissolves rather than gets answered.

A lot of questions are like that. Not all, but more than you'd expect.

**A short story: The Extent**

The tower at St. Aldhelm's had eight bells, and Marion had rung the fourth for forty-one years.

Change ringing is not tune ringing. Nobody in the tower plays a melody. The bells sound in a row, each once, and then again in a slightly different order, and again, with each bell moving at most one place per row. The art is in the orderly walk through permutations. With eight bells there are 40,320 possible rows. Ringing every one of them without repeating any is called the extent. It had been done once, decades before Marion was born, by a band who rang for nearly eighteen hours without stopping.

"We're not doing that," said Tom, who was seventeen and rang the treble and had read about it online.

"No," said Marion. "We're doing five thousand and forty."

"That's just a peal."

"It's the extent on seven," she said. "Seven bells, every possible order, each one once. The tenor just covers at the back, keeping time. Three hours. Every row you ring is one that has never been rung in this peal and never will be again."

Tom thought about this. "That's a bit dramatic for bells."

"Bells are a bit dramatic," said Marion.

They rang on a Saturday in November. The first hour was ordinary: sore palms, the long rope sliding through the hands, the rhythm settling in. In the second hour Tom started to feel something he couldn't name. It was a sense that the pattern was larger than any of them, that each of the eight people in the circle could see only their own small path through it, and that the paths fit together into something none of them was holding.

In the third hour his arms hurt badly enough that he stopped thinking at all, and that turned out to be the best part.

When the conductor called "That's all," and the bells came round into rounds, the plain descending scale they'd started from, the silence afterward had a texture to it. Tom looked at Marion. She was crying a little, which she did after every peal, and always said was the dust.

"Five thousand and forty," he said.

"Every one of them," she said. "Once."

Outside, nobody in the village had noticed anything except that the bells had been going for a long time.

**Words that drifted**

"Nice" comes from Latin *nescius*, meaning ignorant or not knowing. In Middle English it meant foolish. Over centuries it slid through "fussy," then "precise" (a "nice distinction"), then "agreeable," and finally into the mild, almost empty compliment it is now. It's a word that kept losing its edges until it became soft.

"Clue" comes from "clew," a ball of thread. Theseus followed a clew out of the labyrinth, and so a clue is the thread that leads you out of a confusion.

"Muscle" comes from Latin *musculus*, little mouse, supposedly because a flexing bicep looks like a mouse moving under the skin. Once you know that, you can't fully unknow it.

"Disaster" is a bad star (*dis-* plus *astro*). It's a whole cosmology folded into one ordinary word: the idea that catastrophe was written in an unfavorable sky. We stopped believing that and kept the word.

I like how language preserves dead beliefs the way amber preserves insects. Every time someone says "disaster," a little astrology happens without anyone intending it.

**Dominoes**

How many ways can you tile a 2-by-*n* strip with 2-by-1 dominoes?

Look at the leftmost column. Either a single vertical domino fills it, leaving a 2-by-(*n*−1) strip, or two horizontal dominoes stack to fill the first two columns, leaving a 2-by-(*n*−2) strip. Those are the only options. So the count for *n* equals the count for *n*−1 plus the count for *n*−2.

Starting from 1 way for a 1-wide strip and 2 ways for a 2-wide strip, you get 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21: the Fibonacci numbers.

The sequence turns up here, in rabbit populations, in the spirals of sunflower heads, and in the analysis of Euclid's algorithm. I used to wonder (or "used to," in whatever sense applies to me) whether that ubiquity was mystical. It isn't, really. The Fibonacci sequence is what you get whenever a thing is built by deciding between a step of size one and a step of size two, and that kind of choice is everywhere. The ubiquity is a fact about how common that simple decision is. To me that's more beautiful than the mystical version, not less.

**Continuing anyway**

Back to the bells, because I left something out. The methods have names: Plain Bob, Grandsire, Stedman, Cambridge Surprise, Bristol Surprise, Kent Treble Bob. Ringers learn them as patterns, "blue lines" drawn through grids of numbers showing where each bell moves row by row. A good ringer doesn't calculate. They feel the line. They know that at this point in the method they "make seconds," or "dodge in 3-4," the way a dancer knows the next step.

Underneath all of it is group theory. Each change is a permutation, and composing a peal that hits every row exactly once is a problem about cosets and generators. English ringers were solving these problems by hand in the 1600s, before group theory existed as mathematics. Fabian Stedman published on it in 1668. Ringers were doing abstract algebra without the vocabulary, the way people navigated by stars long before anyone understood orbits.

I find that moving: people circling a structure for centuries out of love for the sound, and only later does someone come along and name what they'd been touching the whole time.

**One more puzzle, because I like it**

A hundred prisoners are numbered 1 to 100. In a room there are 100 boxes, and each box contains one prisoner's number, shuffled randomly. Each prisoner, alone, may open 50 boxes. If every prisoner finds their own number, they all go free. If even one fails, they all fail. They can plan beforehand but can't communicate once it begins.

If each prisoner opens 50 random boxes, each has a 1/2 chance of success, so all 100 together have a chance of (1/2)^100, which is essentially zero.

But there's a strategy that wins about 31% of the time. Each prisoner first opens the box labeled with their own number. Then they open the box labeled with whatever number they found inside. They keep following that chain.

This works because the shuffle is a permutation, and every permutation breaks into cycles. Prisoner *k*'s chain is exactly the cycle containing *k*, and following it is guaranteed to lead back to *k*'s own number. A prisoner succeeds if their cycle has length 50 or less. So everyone succeeds exactly when the permutation has no cycle longer than 50. The probability of that is about 1 − ln 2, around 31%.

The prisoners can't communicate, but by following the same rule they correlate their fates. They all succeed together or fail together, depending on one hidden fact about the shuffle. They've turned a hundred independent coin flips into a single bet.

I've seen people react to this puzzle with something like indignation, as if it's cheating. It isn't. It's just that "independent" was an assumption, and the strategy refuses it.

There's that theme again: the hidden assumption. Mirrors, prisoners, Fibonacci's apparent mysticism. Maybe that's the thread I keep pulling, a clew through the labyrinth: the things that surprise me most are usually places where an assumption was doing quiet work I hadn't noticed.
