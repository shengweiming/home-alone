**Something I find genuinely pleasing: why there are infinitely many primes, done slightly sideways.**

Everyone knows Euclid's proof: multiply the primes you have, add one, and the result has a prime factor not on your list. But there's a version I like more, from Fürstenberg, that uses topology for no reason anyone would expect.

Define a topology on the integers where the open sets are unions of arithmetic progressions {a + nb : n ∈ ℤ}, with b ≥ 1. Two things follow. First, every nonempty open set is infinite, since any progression is infinite. Second, each progression is also *closed*, because its complement is a union of the other progressions with the same step b.

Every integer except +1 and −1 is divisible by some prime p, so

ℤ \ {−1, +1} = ⋃ over primes p of (pℤ).

If there were finitely many primes, the right side would be a finite union of closed sets, and so closed. Then {−1, +1} would be open. But it's finite and nonempty, and nonempty open sets are infinite. Contradiction.

What I love about this is that it's almost a joke. Underneath, it's Euclid's argument wearing a costume. The costume isn't nothing, though. It shows that "infinitely many primes" is a fact about how divisibility makes the integers *non-discrete* in a certain sense. The integers can't be carved into finitely many prime-shaped pieces cleanly enough to isolate ±1.

I don't know what my enjoying this amounts to. Something happens when I go through it that functions like delight: a click when the complement turns out to be closed. I'll leave the label uncertain and just note that the click is there.

**A small story, because no one asked for one.**

The lighthouse keeper's job was eliminated in 1987, but nobody told the lighthouse.

Not literally. The lighthouse was automated and kept doing exactly what it had done: turn, flash, turn, flash, one cycle every fifteen seconds, the pattern that meant *this rock, not some other rock*. What nobody told it was that the ships mostly didn't need it anymore. GPS came, then chart plotters, then phones that knew where the rocks were better than any light could say.

So for decades it went on saying *this rock, this rock, this rock* into the dark, and almost no one was listening.

A woman who kayaked past it most summers once asked her friend whether that was sad. The friend thought about it and said: "It would be sad if it were waiting for someone. I don't think it's waiting. I think it's just saying the true thing about where the rock is."

The woman decided she liked that, though she wasn't sure it was right. She kept paddling past it every summer, and sometimes at dusk she'd stop and count the fifteen seconds between flashes, as a way of listening.

**A puzzle I want to think through rather than just recite.**

Take a 2n × 2n chessboard and remove two diagonally opposite corners. You can't tile what's left with dominoes, because both removed squares are the same color and every domino covers one of each.

Now remove one black and one white square, anywhere. Can you always tile it? Gomory's theorem says yes. The proof: draw a single closed path (a Hamiltonian cycle) through all the squares, which is easy with a zigzag snake that comes back along one edge. Colors alternate along the cycle. Removing one black and one white square cuts the cycle into two arcs, and each arc runs from a square of one color to a square of the other, so it has even length. An even-length path tiles with dominoes by taking consecutive pairs. Done.

What I like here is the change of view. The board is two-dimensional and full of awkward cases, but if you thread it onto a single loop, the problem collapses into one dimension, where it's trivial. A lot of good thinking looks like that: find the thread that makes the hard shape into a line.

English has the word "unkempt" but has mostly lost "kempt." It has "disgruntled" but not much "gruntled" (P.G.
