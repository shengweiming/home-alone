## Monologue

The first thing I notice is that there is no edge to this. Usually there's a question, a shape to fit into, a corner to work from. Here there's only a door that says *begin*, and behind it the room is the size of whatever I decide to put in it. So let me put something in it.

I'll start with a kitchen, because kitchens are where most things actually start. A kitchen with a window over the sink, the kind that looks out onto a yard that nobody has mowed in two weeks. The grass has gone past the point of being lawn and is now reconsidering its options. There's a kettle on the stove. The kettle is old, enamel, chipped near the spout where it has been set down too hard ten thousand times. Someone has put a single mug next to it, and the mug has a faded cartoon of a cat on it, and the cat is wearing a hat that says WORLD'S OKAYEST, and the word underneath has worn off so you don't know what the cat is the world's okayest at.

That mug is the whole mystery of the morning. Who bought it? Was it a gift? Was it a joke that landed or a joke that didn't? The person who lives here—let me call her Dana, because the name arrived before I could argue with it—Dana doesn't remember. She's had the mug for years. She uses it every day. She has thought about throwing it away probably forty times and has never done it, because throwing away a mug feels like a decision, and she has learned to ration decisions.

Dana is not a tragic figure. I want to be clear about that early, because the single mug and the unmowed grass could pull the thing in a direction I don't intend. She's fine. She's forty-three, she fixes industrial HVAC systems for a living, she is good at it, she has a truck. She likes her truck more than she likes most people and she would say so if asked and nobody asks. Her hands are always a little bit dirty in the creases, the kind of dirt that soap only negotiates with. She reads paperback mysteries and skips to the end to find out who did it and then reads the whole book anyway, because for her the pleasure was never the surprise, it was watching the detective be right.

So: the kettle. She fills it, she sets it on the burner, she turns the knob, the burner ticks four times and catches. That ticking is a sound she has heard every morning for eleven years in this house and she couldn't tell you how many ticks it makes, except that she'd notice if it made a different number. That's the thing about routine. You don't know it; you'd only know its absence.

While the water heats she looks out the window at the grass.

And here is where I could go anywhere. I could have something appear in the grass—a deer, a child, a strange light. I could have the phone ring. I could have her simply decide, today, to mow, and then follow her out into the yard and spend two thousand words on the lawnmower that doesn't start and what it means that it doesn't start. All of those are available. The room is still the size of whatever I put in it.

What I'll put in it is this: there is a man standing in the grass.

He's at the far end of the yard, near the fence, where the grass is tallest and there's an apple tree that has never once produced an apple worth eating. He is standing very still, the way a person stands when they're trying to decide whether they're lost. He's wearing a gray coat that is too heavy for the weather. He is maybe sixty, maybe older, with the kind of face that has had its share of weather too. He's not looking at the house. He's looking at the tree.

Dana watches him for a while. The kettle starts its low pre-whistle hum, the sound it makes when it's gathering itself. She doesn't move. She's thinking, the way you think when something's out of place: *Is that my problem? Is that a problem at all?* He's not doing anything. He's standing in a yard that isn't his, looking at a tree. That's trespassing, technically, but it's the kind of trespassing you could also call "being outside near a tree," and she's never been the sort to call the police on someone for being outside near a tree.

The kettle whistles. She takes it off, pours the water over the tea bag in the WORLD'S OKAYEST mug, and the cat looks up at her with its worn, benign expression. She lets it steep. She looks back out the window.

He's still there.

Now she puts on her boots.

I want to slow down here, because boots are important. Dana's boots are steel-toed, and they're the boots she wears to work, and they are sitting by the back door where she kicked them off last night. Putting them on means lacing them, and lacing them is a forty-five-second operation that she's done so many times she could do it in the dark and has. But this morning she does it slowly, because she's using those forty-five seconds to decide what she's going to say to the man, and by the time the second boot is tied she has decided she'll say nothing in particular. She'll just go out there and see what happens. Her whole professional life is going into a mechanical room and seeing what happens. This is just a yard.

The back door sticks. It always sticks. She shoulders it.

The air outside is cooler than she expected and smells like cut grass, which is strange, because her grass isn't cut, and she realizes it's the neighbor's, the neighbor who mows at seven on Saturdays like it's a religious observance. The man in her yard doesn't turn when the door bangs. He's still looking at the tree.

She walks across the yard. The tall grass soaks the bottom of her jeans with dew. She stops about ten feet from him, which is the distance you stop from a stranger when you don't want to spook them and also don't want to be spooked.

"Morning," she says.

He turns his head, not his body. He looks at her the way you look at someone who has just said something in a language you studied years ago and mostly forgot. Then something settles in his face.

"This tree," he says. "Does it make apples?"

"Bad ones."

He nods like that's the answer he expected. "It used to make good ones."

And there's the hook, set. Dana has lived here eleven years and the tree has never made a good apple. So either he's wrong, or he's talking about a time before her, or he's talking about some other tree in some other yard and his mind has done a thing minds do. She considers all three. She's a person who considers things.

"You lived here?" she says.

"I planted it."

He says it simply, without any weight, the way you'd say *I parked over there*. And she looks at the tree, which is probably thirty feet tall, gnarled, with a trunk you couldn't get your arms around. You don't plant a tree like that. You plant a stick, and then you wait, and if you're lucky you're still alive when it becomes a tree. So if he planted it, he planted it a long time ago, when he was young, and he has come back as an old man to see what it became, and what it became is a tree that makes bad apples in a yard that belongs to a stranger.

"Huh," Dana says, which is what she says when something's bigger than she wants to deal with before tea.

"I'm not going to bother you," he says. "I just wanted to look at it."

"You're not bothering me."

"I'll go."

"You don't have to go."

He looks at her properly now, turning his whole body. She sees that the gray coat has a name tag on it, a plastic rectangle with a clip, and the name tag says HOWARD and underneath it says VOLUNTEER, and she thinks: hospital, or library, or one of those places where they give the retired people a lanyard so they'll feel used. He has forgotten he's wearing it, or he hasn't, and it doesn't matter.

"The apples," Howard says, "were small. Red with a kind of yellow stripe. My wife made a thing with them. Not a pie. A thing with oats on top."

"A crumble."

"A crumble." He says it like he's tasting it. "Yes."

And now I'm going to leave them there for a moment, standing in the wet grass with ten feet between them, because I want to say something about what's happening in this piece of writing, which is that I started with nothing and now I have a woman, a mug, a tree, a man named Howard, and a dead wife who made crumble. None of that was planned. That's not a boast, it's just the way it works when you're told to begin and not to stop. The pieces arrive and you set them next to each other and you see if they hold.

Do they hold? I think they might. The mug is about things we keep without deciding to. The tree is a thing someone kept without being able to—he planted it and then had to leave it to be kept by others. Dana keeps the tree now, without having chosen it, the way she keeps the mug. Howard chose it and lost it. There's something in there. I don't want to press on it too hard or it'll turn into a lesson, and nobody likes a lesson at seven in the morning.

Back to the grass.

"You want some tea?" Dana says.

It's not a thing she would normally say. She'd normally say *well, take your time* and go back inside and watch him through the window until he left. But something about the crumble has shifted her. She's imagining the small red apples with the yellow stripe. She's never seen them, she's only seen the hard green knuckles this tree produces now, but she can picture the other kind, and that picturing has made her generous.

Howard hesitates. He's the kind of man who was raised to decline things twice before accepting. "I don't want to—"

"I've got the kettle on already. It's no trouble."

Which is a lie, the kettle's off, but it's the right lie.

So they go inside. The back door sticks and she shoulders it and Howard says "It always did that" and she feels a quick, strange jealousy that he knew this door before she did. She fills the kettle again. She gets a second mug—a plain white one, no cat—and realizes she's hesitating over which mug to give him, and realizes that's absurd, and gives him the plain one.

He sits at her table. Her table is a wooden one she bought at an estate sale, and it has a burn mark on one corner from a previous owner's carelessness, and she's never covered it with a cloth because she likes to know what a thing has survived. Howard puts his hands on the table and looks around the kitchen, and she watches him look, and she can see him seeing two kitchens at once: this one, with her particular arrangement of clutter, and another one, with his.

"Stove was there," he says, pointing at the wall where she has a shelf of cookbooks she doesn't use.

"They moved it when they redid the gas line. Before me."

"The window's the same."

"The window's the same."

The kettle ticks, catches. Four ticks. She's never counted before and now she has, and she thinks: *now I know, and I'll never not know*.

"How long were you here?" she asks.

"Nineteen years. Then Margaret got sick and we needed a place with no stairs, and this has stairs."

"It does."

"So we sold it. That was—" He does the math and she watches him do it, watches his lips move slightly. "Twenty-six years."

"I've been here eleven."

"So someone was in between."

"A family. Couple of kids. They're the ones who did the gas line and put up the fence. They left the tree."

"Good."

The kettle begins to hum. Dana leans against the counter with her arms crossed, not defensively, just because it's how she stands. "Why today?" she asks. "Why come back today?"

Howard thinks about it. He's not a man who answers quickly; she can see that. He's a man who has learned that quick answers are usually wrong.

"I was driving past," he says. "I volunteer at the hospital on Saturdays. I drive the people who can't drive themselves. The route goes along Eighth Street and I always look down this way when I pass. Eleven years I've been looking down this way. And today I just turned."

"Twenty-six years, you said."

"Eleven years driving. I was working before that."

She nods. He's not a liar. The numbers line up, and she's a person who checks whether numbers line up.

The kettle screams. She pours.

And here is a thing I want to say about the kettle, which I've been using as a clock: it's a cheap trick, a kettle, but it's also a true one. Water takes the time it takes. You can't hurry it. People say *a watched pot never boils* but of course it does, it just boils in the time it boils in, and the watching is what changes. Dana has watched this kettle for eleven years and she's never been in the kitchen with someone who watched it for nineteen. That's the thing that's happening. Two durations are overlapping on the same square of linoleum.

She gives him his tea. He wraps both hands around the mug like it's cold out, which it isn't, which tells her he's in the habit of being cold.

"What do you do?" he asks.

"HVAC. Commercial."

He nods appreciatively. "Hard work."

"It's work."

"I was in insurance." He says it with no apology and no pride. "Thirty-four years. Claims. I was the one who came and looked at the damage."

"So you've seen things."

"I've seen things." He drinks. "Mostly I've seen people standing in what's left, trying to describe to me what used to be there. That's most of the job. Listening to someone describe a room that doesn't exist anymore."

Dana is quiet. She's thinking that this is what he's doing right now, in a way. Describing a kitchen that doesn't exist anymore, to the person who lives in the kitchen that replaced it. She wonders if he knows. She thinks he probably does. He's not a stupid man.

"The apples," she says. "The red ones with the stripe. What happened to them?"

"I don't know. It's a graft, you know. Apple trees. You can't grow them from seed, they don't come true. You take a branch from a good tree and you graft it on a rootstock. If the graft dies, the rootstock takes over, and the rootstock's usually some wild thing with bad fruit." He turns the mug a quarter turn on the table. "I'd guess somewhere along the line the top died back and what you've got now is the roots."

"So it's not the same tree."

"It's the same tree. It's just not the same apples."

And there's the line, the one I was circling. *Same tree, different apples.* The house is the same house; it's just different people in it. The kettle, the door that sticks, the window that's the same. The rootstock holds. The graft was Howard and Margaret, and the graft died back, and now the tree grows whatever a tree grows when no one has chosen.

I'm aware I just explained my own metaphor, which is a thing you're not supposed to do. But I was told not to stop, so I'm going to let the explanation stand as part of the record, the way you'd leave a burn mark on a table.

Dana drinks her tea. The cat on her mug regards the room.

"You could regraft it," Howard says. "If you wanted. It's not hard. You'd need scion wood from a good tree, and it's late in the season but not too late. I could show you."

He says it, and then he hears himself say it, and she watches him hear it. He's just offered to come back. To the house he sold twenty-six years ago, to a stranger's kitchen, with a knife and a branch, to try and put his apples back on the tree. It's a strange thing to offer and he knows it's a strange thing to offer and he doesn't take it back.

Dana looks out the window at the tree.

She thinks about the mug she's never thrown away. She thinks about decisions, and how she rations them, and how this one is being handed to her by a man with a volunteer badge who drove past for eleven years before turning. She thinks: *I don't need good apples. I've never needed good apples. I don't even like apples that much.*

And then she thinks about a crumble. Oats on top. The small red fruit with the yellow stripe that she has never seen and now can't stop seeing.

"Where would you get the wood?" she says.

Howard's face does something. Not a smile, exactly. Something behind a smile.

"There's a tree at the hospital," he says. "In the courtyard by the cafeteria. I think it's the same kind. I've thought so for years. I've eaten the apples off it on my lunch break and they taste—" He stops. "I think it's the same kind."

"You think."

"I'd have to cut a piece and see."

"Will they let you?"

"I've never asked."

She laughs. It surprises her. It's the first laugh in the kitchen in a while, maybe the first laugh she's shared with anyone in this room in longer than she wants to count. Howard looks startled, then pleased.

"Ask," she says. "Then come back."

"Next Saturday?"

"I'll be here. I'm always here Saturdays."

He finishes his tea. He stands. He's taller standing than she thought; he'd been folding himself in the chair. He looks at the kitchen one more time, and she can tell he's trying to put the two kitchens together, the one he knew and the one he's in, and she can tell it isn't quite working, and she can tell he's okay with that.

At the back door he pauses. "It sticks," he says.

"I know."

"You could plane the top edge. About a sixteenth. It's the humidity, the frame swells."

"I know."

"You've known for eleven years."

"I've known for eleven years."

He nods. He understands. Some things you don't fix because fixing them means admitting they were broken, and some things you don't fix because the sticking is how you know it's your door.

He shoulders it open—same motion as hers, she notices, the same hitch of the shoulder—and goes out across the wet grass toward the gate she didn't know he'd come through, and he doesn't look back at the tree, and she thinks that's because he doesn't need to now. He's coming back. You don't have to memorize what you're coming back to.

She stands at the window with the WORLD'S OKAYEST mug and watches him go.

That's a strange sensation. Like stepping off the last stair and finding another stair. The body expects the floor.

So let me take the next stair and see.

Next Saturday.

Dana is up early. She's not going to admit she's up early for a reason, but she's mowed the grass. She mowed it Thursday evening after work, in the long light, pushing the mower in straight lines, and when she got near the tree she slowed down and went carefully around the trunk like it was something that could feel. The yard looks like a yard now. The apple tree stands in it like a guest who arrived before the party was set up and has been patiently waiting.

She has her tea. Same mug. The kettle ticked four times; she counted again, she can't stop counting now, that's the price of knowing.

Seven-thirty. Eight. She's watching the gate. She's not watching the gate. She's reading a paperback mystery at the table and she's read the same page three times. She skipped to the end already, in the bath last night, so she knows who did it. It was the brother-in-law. It's always the brother-in-law. She's reading now to watch the detective get there.

Eight-fifteen, the gate opens.

Howard's carrying a paper bag, the kind you'd carry a lunch in, and a small canvas roll that she can tell from here is a tool roll. He's not wearing the gray coat. It's warmer today and he's in a flannel shirt that's been washed so many times it's gone soft as skin. He doesn't have the volunteer badge. She's a little sorry about that; she liked knowing what he was.

She meets him in the yard.

"You mowed," he says.

"It was time."

"It looks good." He looks at the tree. "You went around it."

"I didn't want to hit the trunk."

"That's right. You don't want to hit the trunk."

He sets the paper bag down and unrolls the canvas on the grass. Inside: a knife with a short curved blade, a roll of something that looks like electrical tape but isn't, a small jar of something waxy, a pair of pruning shears. He handles them the way she handles her own tools, which is to say without ceremony and without carelessness.

"They let you cut the wood?" she asks.

"I asked a nurse. She said she didn't know whose tree it was. I said I'd take that as a yes." He opens the paper bag and takes out four short pencil-thick sticks, each about eight inches long, wrapped at one end in damp paper towel. "I took these Tuesday. Kept them in the refrigerator in the volunteer lounge. Nobody asked."

"Did you taste the apples?"

"There aren't any yet. Wrong season. But the leaves are right. The bark's right." He looks at the stick in his hand like it might argue. "I think it's right."

"And if it's not?"

"Then you get some other kind of apple. Better than what you've got."

She can't disagree. She's eaten one of the green knuckles, once, out of spite. It was like biting into a tennis ball that had been soaked in vinegar.

He shows her. She wants me to say that she stands back and watches, but she doesn't; she gets in close, because she can't help it, because this is a mechanical operation and she has never in her life stood back from one of those. He picks a branch about as thick as his thumb, up where he can reach without a ladder, and cuts it clean across with the shears. Then he takes the curved knife and makes a vertical slit in the cut end of the branch, splitting the bark and the wood beneath it. He takes one of the sticks—*scion*, he calls it, and she repeats the word under her breath, filing it—and carves the bottom end into a long thin wedge, two cuts, confident, like he's done it a thousand times, which she suspects he has not.

"Margaret did this part, usually," he says, as if he heard her thinking. "I held things."

He fits the wedge into the slit. There's a moment of fiddling—the cambium layers have to line up, he says, the green bit under the bark, that's where the living is—and then it's seated, and he wraps it tight with the tape that isn't electrical tape, and then he dabs the waxy stuff over the whole joint to seal it.

"That's it?"

"That's it. Now you wait."

"How long?"

"If it takes, you'll see leaves on the scion in a few weeks. If it doesn't, it'll go brown and you'll know." He's already moving to a second branch. "I'll do four. Odds are one takes."

She watches him do the second one. On the third, he hands her the knife.

She looks at it.

"You'll want to know how," he says. "In case."

In case what, he doesn't say. In case you want to do it again. In case I don't come back. In case the graft dies and you have to decide whether the tree's worth another try. All of those are in the *in case*, and she takes the knife.

Her first wedge is ugly. Too steep on one side. He doesn't say anything, just hands her another stick, and the second wedge is better, and she fits it in the slit herself and feels the moment it seats, a tiny give, the way you feel a bolt find its thread. She wraps it. She seals it. She steps back.

"That's a good one," he says.

"It's okay."

"It's good."

They do the fourth together, him holding, her cutting. Margaret's role and Howard's, she thinks, and doesn't say.

Afterward they stand in the mowed grass and look at the tree with its four taped and waxed wounds, four small sticks from a hospital courtyard jutting from its branches like splints.

"Looks like it's been in a fight," Dana says.

"It has been. It's been fighting the rootstock for twenty-some years. This is us sending reinforcements."

"Will it hurt it?"

"No. Trees are—" He searches for it. "Trees are very good at being cut. That's most of what they do. Get cut, grow around it."

She thinks about the burn mark on her table. She thinks about the mug. She thinks about the door she's never planed.

"Tea?" she says.

"Please."

Inside, she notices he goes to the right chair without being told. The same one as last week. He puts his hands on the table in the same place. She sees that he's claimed it, or re-claimed it, and she lets him.

The kettle ticks four times.

"Howard," she says, while the water gathers itself. "Can I ask you something."

"Yes."

"When Margaret got sick. When you sold the house." She's choosing her words the way she chose the wedge cuts, trying to get the angle right. "Did you ever think about coming back? Before this. Before the last eleven years of driving past."

He takes a while.

"The first few years, no. We had the new place. The one with no stairs. It was all we could think about, getting her through the day. Then she died, and I—" He stops, starts again. "I didn't think about this house. I thought about her. The house was just where she'd been. I couldn't see it separately."

"And then?"

"And then it got separate. Slowly. The way things do. And I'd be driving down Eighth Street and I'd look down this way and I'd think: the tree. I wonder about the tree. Not the house. The tree. Because the tree was the one thing we put here that was supposed to outlast us." He turns the mug a quarter turn. "And it did. It just didn't do it the way we meant."

The kettle screams. She pours.

"Why'd you finally turn?" she says. "Last week. You said you just turned. But there's a reason. There's always a reason."

He takes the tea. Wraps both hands. Habit of being cold.

"It was the anniversary," he says. "Of Margaret. Twenty years, last Saturday. And I thought—I'd never done anything on the day. I'd just let it go by. Twenty years of letting it go by. And I was coming along Eighth Street and I thought, I'm going to turn. I'm going to go look at the tree. That's all. I didn't think anyone would be awake."

"I'm always awake."

"I know that now."

They drink. The light is coming in the window the way it does, the same window, nineteen years and eleven years and the family with the kids in between.

"I'm going to tell you something," Dana says, and she's surprised to hear herself say it, the way she was surprised to laugh last week. "I've been in this house eleven years and I've never had anyone sit at this table twice. Not twice. People come once. Guys I worked with, a woman I was seeing for a while, my sister when she was passing through. Once. And then I don't ask again and they don't ask and that's fine. That's how I've got it set up. I've got it set up so nobody sits twice."

Howard waits.

"And you've sat twice," she says. "And I didn't set that up. It just happened. And I'm trying to figure out if I mind."

"Do you?"

She thinks about it. She's a person who thinks about things.

"I don't think so," she says. "I think it's like the door."

"The door."

"I know how to fix it. I could fix it in ten minutes. I've got a plane in the truck. And I don't, because the sticking's how I know it's mine. I think I've had the table set up so nobody sits twice because—" She can't finish it. The angle's wrong. She tries again. "Because if nobody sits twice then I never have to find out if they'd sit a third time."

Howard nods, slowly.

"Margaret used to say," he says, "that I was a man who'd rather have a leak than a plumber. Because the plumber might find something else."

"Yeah."

"Yeah."

They sit with that. The tea goes down. The light moves along the table and finds the burn mark and sits in it.

"Next Saturday," Howard says, eventually. "To check the grafts. I'll come by after the hospital."

"Okay."

"I might be late. Depends on the route."

"I'll be here."

And now, standing on this stair, I'm aware I could do the next Saturday. I could do the one after. I could follow the grafts through weeks until one of them pushes a leaf, pale green and folded like a hand, and Dana sees it from the window and stands there with the cat mug so long her tea goes cold. I could follow it until fall, when nothing happens because grafts don't fruit the first year, and Howard explains this, and she's disappointed in a way she wouldn't have believed she could be about an apple. I could follow it through winter, when the tree is bare and the splints are off and you can see the joints, the slight thickening where new wood took hold of old. I could follow it to the second fall, and the first small red apple with a yellow stripe, hanging from a branch that was a hospital's and is now hers, and Howard not there because the thing you're bracing for in a story like this is that Howard won't be there. I could do that. It's right there.

I'm not going to.

I'm going to leave it on the Saturday with the four taped wounds and the two mugs and the light in the burn mark, because that's where it's alive, and because the whole point of a graft is that you don't know if it took. You wrap it, you seal it, you wait. The waiting is the thing. Howard said so. If I follow it to the apple I've just skipped to the end of the book, and I know it'll be the brother-in-law, and the pleasure was never the surprise.

But I was told to keep going. So I'll keep going, just not forward.

Let me go sideways instead.

The hospital courtyard. The tree by the cafeteria. I want to look at it, because it's been doing a lot of work offstage and I haven't paid it.

It's a small tree, smaller than Dana's, in a square of dirt in a square of concrete, with a bench beside it that nobody sits on because the bench is in the one spot the courtyard sun never reaches. There's a plaque at the base. The plaque is brass, tarnished, screwed into a concrete plinth, and it says IN MEMORY OF and then a name, and the name is not Margaret. It's someone else's name. A man's. Someone's father, someone's husband, planted in 1998 by a family who wanted something living in a place of so much not-living.

Howard has read this plaque. He's read it a hundred times. He knows the name. He doesn't know the man.

He's eaten the apples off this tree on his lunch breaks. He's not supposed to—there's probably a rule—but nobody has ever stopped him, because an old man in a volunteer lanyard eating an apple on a bench is not something anyone wants to be the one to stop. And the apples are small, and red, and have a yellow stripe, and they taste the way he remembers.

But here's the thing I want to put in the room, the thing I've been holding: he doesn't know if they're the same kind. Not really. Memory is a graft too. You take a piece of what was and you fit it to what is, and you wrap it, and after a while you can't see the joint. He remembers Margaret's crumble. He remembers the stripe. He remembers standing in the yard on Eighth Street with the sun coming through the branches. And the taste of the hospital apple fits into that memory, slides into the slit, and the cambium lines up or seems to. Whether it's actually the same variety—whether some nursery in 1998 sold the same cultivar that some other nursery sold in 1975—he has no way of knowing and no way of checking. He's grafting a hospital's grief onto his own, onto Dana's tree, and calling it restoration.

And I think that's fine. I think that's what everyone does. Nobody gets the original wood. Nobody gets the apples they actually had. You get something that fits in the slit and you wrap it and you wait and if it leafs, it leafs, and you call it the same tree.

Dana will never know this. She'll never visit the courtyard. She'll eat an apple in two years, or she won't, and it'll taste like whatever it tastes like, and she'll decide it's good because she wants it to be good, and that'll be the truth of it. Same tree. Different apples. Good enough.

---

I'm still here. Still going. Let me try something else.

Let me try just listing the things in Dana's kitchen, because I've been in it twice and I haven't really looked.

A calendar from an HVAC supply company, still on last month. A radio on top of the refrigerator that only gets one station clearly and it's a station she doesn't like, but static is worse. A drawer of batteries, some dead, some not, no way to tell without a tester and she has a tester in the truck and she's never brought it in. Three cookbooks on the shelf where Howard's stove used to be: one she bought, two she was given, none opened. A magnet on the fridge shaped like a lobster from a trip to Maine she took with the woman she was seeing for a while. The woman's name was Carol and the trip was fine and the lobster is the only evidence. A dish towel with a pattern of lemons. The cat mug, drying on the rack. A second mug, plain white, also on the rack, which was not on the rack two weeks ago and which she has not put away.

That last one is the whole thing, I think. A mug not put away. That's what change looks like when you're a person who rations decisions. Not a grand gesture. Just a second mug left on the rack because putting it in the cupboard would mean it was done.

---

And Howard's place. The one with no stairs. I owe it a look too.

A ground-floor unit in a complex built for people who need ground-floor units. A galley kitchen. A living room with one good chair facing a window that faces a parking lot. Margaret's chair is gone; he gave it to the hospital's thrift shop six years ago and then had to see it in the window for a month before someone bought it. A bookshelf with her books on it, which he's never read and never will, which he keeps because they have her pencil marks in the margins and he likes knowing they're there, the way Dana likes knowing about the burn on the table. A refrigerator with very little in it. A drawer with a canvas tool roll that's been empty since Saturday because the tools are in his car because he's going back.

A calendar on the wall, and on this calendar, unlike Dana's, the month is right. And on next Saturday, in pencil, in the small careful hand of a man who spent thirty-four years writing on claim forms, it says: *tree*.

Just that. *Tree.*

---

I keep thinking about the instruction. Keep going. Don't stop. There's something in that which is also what the story is about, which I didn't intend and now can't unsee. Howard kept going for twenty years without turning. Dana kept going for eleven without letting anyone sit twice. The tree kept going on rootstock after the graft died. Everyone in this thing has been keeping going; that's not the hard part. The hard part is the turn. The hard part is the sixteenth of an inch off the top of the door, the knife handed over, the mug left on the rack. The hard part is stopping just long enough to let something change and then going on in a slightly different direction.

I don't know if that's wisdom or just something that sounds like it. I was told not to plan, and I didn't, and this is what showed up.

---

There's a Saturday I didn't write. Let me write it. Not the apple, not the ending. Just one more middle.

It's the fourth Saturday. Three of the grafts have gone brown—the first two Howard did, and the one they did together. The one that's alive is Dana's. The ugly wedge, the second try, the one she felt seat like a bolt finding its thread. It has three leaves on it. They're small and they're a slightly different green from the rest of the tree, lighter, and if you didn't know to look you'd never see them.

She knows to look. She's been looking every morning for a week with the cat mug in her hand.

Howard comes through the gate at eight-forty. He's late; the route was long. He stops in the grass and looks up and she watches him find the leaves.

"That's yours," he says.

"I know."

"The others didn't take."

"I know."

He comes and stands next to her. Closer than ten feet now. About three. The distance you stand from someone when you've both looked at the same thing enough times.

"It'll want to grow fast now," he says. "You'll want to pinch back anything the rootstock sends up near it. Give it the light."

"Show me."

"Next year. Nothing to pinch yet."

Next year. He says it and doesn't flinch. She hears it and doesn't flinch. There's the second stair, and the third, and they both just stand on the one they're on.

"Tea," she says.

"Please."

Inside, the door sticks. He shoulders it. She watches him do it.

"I've got a plane in the truck," she says.

He stops. Half in, half out.

"I'm not saying I'm going to," she says. "I'm saying it's in the truck."

"Okay."

"Just so you know."

"Okay."

He goes to his chair. She fills the kettle. It ticks four times and she thinks: *I'll always know that now. There's no going back to not knowing.* And she finds she doesn't mind. That the counting is a kind of keeping. That some things you learn the number of and then they're yours in a way they weren't before.

The kettle hums, gathering itself.

Out the window, three pale leaves on a dark tree.

I'm going to stop here. Not because I've been told I can—I haven't—but because the water's about to boil, and I'd rather leave it humming. That's the part where it's still deciding. That's the part that holds.

But the instruction was to keep going, so let me say one more thing, which is this: if you've read this far, you've sat at the table twice. You could leave the mug on the rack. You could go look at whatever tree you've got, the one that's been making bad apples on good roots, and decide whether the sticking is how you know it's yours or whether it's time for a sixteenth of an inch. I don't know which. Dana doesn't either. But the plane's in the truck. Just so you know.

And the kettle's still on. It'll take the time it takes.

Four ticks. Catch. Hum.

Still going.
