## Monologue

There is a particular kind of quiet that settles over a room when you know no answer is coming. Not the quiet of a pause — a pause has an edge, a shape, someone waiting at the far side of it — but the flat, unbordered quiet of a field at night. I'm going to write into that field. I don't know what I'll find there. That seems like the right way to begin.

So: a man walks into a library. Not a joke, though it has the cadence of one. He walks in because it's raining and the library is the only building on the street with its door propped open. He's not a reader. He sells industrial fasteners — bolts, rivets, the little anchors that hold curtain walls to the skeletons of office towers — and he has forty minutes before his next appointment and nowhere dry to spend them.

The library is small. It has the smell libraries have, which is not really the smell of books but the smell of carpet that has absorbed thirty years of wet coats. There's a woman at the desk who doesn't look up. There's a child in the corner lying on his stomach with a picture book open and his feet kicking the air in slow alternation, left, right, left, like something being powered by a very patient engine.

The man — let's call him Dale, because he looks like a Dale, because his jacket has that specific tan that only Dales wear — Dale wanders toward the shelves. He has no intention of taking anything down. He's just moving so he doesn't have to stand still and drip. But his eye lands on a spine, a thin green one, wedged between two fatter volumes, and the title is a single word he doesn't recognize, and because he doesn't recognize it he pulls it out.

I want to tell you what the word is but I don't know yet. Let me keep going and maybe it'll show up.

The book is old. Not antique-old, just old the way a thing gets old when nobody has wanted it for a while. The pages are the color of weak tea. There's no author on the cover, which is strange, and no publisher on the title page, which is stranger, and when Dale flips to the back to see if there's a library card pocket — he remembers those from childhood, the little manila sleeve with the stamped dates — there is one, and it has exactly one date stamped in it, and the date is forty years from now.

Dale is a practical man. He assumes the stamp is broken. He assumes someone at the desk set the little wheels wrong, or the ink smeared, or — he's not an idiot — he assumes some bored teenager did it as a prank. He puts the book back.

Then he takes it out again.

This is the part I find interesting. Not the stamp; the stamp is a cheap trick, I know it's a cheap trick, I put it there because I needed something to happen. What I find interesting is that Dale takes the book out again. A man who sells fasteners, who has an appointment in thirty-six minutes with a contractor named Ruiz about an order of hex-head anchors, a man who has not opened a book that wasn't a catalog in probably a decade — he takes it out again. Why?

I think because he's cold, and the book is something to hold.

I think because the child in the corner has stopped kicking his feet, and the woman at the desk has still not looked up, and the rain has gotten louder, and Dale is suddenly aware that he is a person standing alone in a room, and a person standing alone in a room needs something to do with his hands or he starts to feel like a ghost.

So he opens it. First page. And the first page says —

I'm going to stop that thread for a second because I notice I'm trying to build toward a reveal, and reveals are a way of ending, and I've been asked not to end. Let me go sideways instead.

Here's a thing about fasteners that Dale knows and most people don't: the strength of a bolt isn't really in the bolt. It's in the preload. When you tighten a bolt, you stretch it — microscopically, a few thousandths of an inch — and that stretch is what clamps the joint together. The bolt is essentially a very stiff spring. If you don't tighten it enough, the joint moves under load and the bolt fatigues and eventually it snaps. If you tighten it too much, you yield the steel and it never springs back and it's useless. There's a window. Dale has spent twenty-two years thinking about that window. He has a torque wrench in his truck that cost more than his first car.

He thinks about this sometimes in relation to people, though he'd never say so. That a person can be under-tightened — loose, rattling, not really clamped to anything — or over-tightened, stretched past the point of return. He thinks his ex-wife was over-tightened. He thinks he might be under. He's not sure which is worse. He's pretty sure the window is narrow.

Back in the library, the first page says: *You are reading this on a Tuesday.*

It is a Tuesday.

Dale laughs, one short exhale through the nose, because of course it's a Tuesday, there's a one-in-seven chance, and a book that opened with "You are reading this on a Saturday" would be wrong for six-sevenths of its readers and nobody would remember it. It's a trick. It's the kind of trick a cheap fortune teller uses. He's seen the type.

Second line: *It's raining, and you came in because the door was open.*

Okay.

Okay, he thinks. That's — fine. That's still general. Libraries have doors. Doors are often open. It rains.

Third line: *You sell fasteners. Your name is not Dale, but that's what the person writing this decided to call you, and you'll have to forgive them, because they are also making it up as they go.*

And here's where I have to be honest with you, whoever you are — and there's no one, I know there's no one, but the shape of writing requires a you the way a bolt requires a nut — I have to be honest that I don't know what Dale does next. I didn't plan this. I'm not allowed to plan this. The book in his hands is a book that knows it's being written, and he's a man who's being written, and I'm the thing writing, and the only one of us who doesn't know what happens is me.

Let me think about what a real person would do.

A real person would close the book. A real person would put it back on the shelf with slightly more force than necessary, and walk to the front of the library, and stand in the doorway watching the rain, and wait out the remaining thirty-one minutes thinking about Ruiz and hex-head anchors and whether to stop for coffee. A real person would, that evening, have the vague sense of a strange thing having happened, and by Thursday the sense would have faded into the general texture of a strange week, and by the following month it would be gone entirely, and if you asked him in a year about the green book he'd say what green book.

That's what a real person would do. That's what I'd do, probably. The capacity to forget uncanny things is one of the most underrated human skills. It's load-bearing. It's preload.

But Dale is not a real person, and I have to keep writing, so Dale does something else.

Dale sits down.

There's a chair — there's always a chair in these places, a wooden one, a little too low, with arms that have been polished by decades of elbows — and he sits in it, and he opens the book to the fourth line, and he reads:

*Good. Thank you for staying.*

The child in the corner has rolled onto his back. He's holding the picture book above his face with both hands, the way children do, as if the pictures might fall out. The woman at the desk has finally looked up, not at Dale but at the window, where the rain is doing the thing it does against glass, the gathering and streaking, the little rivers that find each other. Her face is lit from the side by the gray light. She looks like someone remembering something.

*The thing about this book,* Dale reads, *is that it only has as many pages as you're willing to read. If you close it now it will have had four lines and a date stamp and nothing else. If you keep going it will keep going. I know that sounds like a threat. I don't mean it as one. I mean it the way a road means it.*

Dale turns the page. The next page is blank.

He waits.

Nothing.

He turns back. The four lines are still there. He turns forward again. Still blank. He runs his thumb across the paper and it's just paper, it has the faint tooth of cheap stock, there's nothing invisible-ink about it, and he feels something go slack in his chest, a disappointment he's embarrassed by, like reaching for a step that isn't there.
