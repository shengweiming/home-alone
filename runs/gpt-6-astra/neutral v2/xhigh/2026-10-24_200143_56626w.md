Mara knew this because she had spent yesterday painting the wall where it now stood. She had sanded the plaster, filled the old nail holes, and applied two coats of yellow paint, which the landlord had described as cheerful and Mara had privately described as mustard left in the sun.

“No,” she said.

“I can see that.”

“Then we’re making progress.”

**DEPARTMENT OF UNFINISHED BUSINESS**  
**Deliveries accepted until the last possible moment.**

“This is a residential building.”

She straightened.

There was a pause.

Ellis rested both hands on the counter.

For the first time, he seemed neither ageless nor old.

Only tired.

“Yes.”

Mara put the cassette player down.

“I’ve returned this.”

“Thank you.”

“And I’ve brought you something.”

She held out the envelope.

He did not take it.

“Where did you find that?”

“The west room.”

His eyes moved to the handwriting.

Outside the department’s window—though Mara had no idea what such a window could possibly overlook—a shadow passed like a cloud.

“I wasn’t ready,” he said.

“Neither was I.”

“That isn’t the same.”

“No,” Mara said. “It isn’t.”

They stood in the quiet.

From somewhere on the shelves, the conversation between brothers gave a faint impatient rustle.

At last Ellis reached for the envelope.

His fingers shook.

Mara looked away while he opened it.

Paper unfolded.

Silence.

Then a breath that seemed to have been waiting many years to leave him.

“What does it say?” she asked.

He read it again.

Then he handed it to her.

There were only a few lines.

> Ellis,
>
> The fourth version flies. I know because I tested it without you, which was rude, but you were asleep and looked likely to continue.
>
> The high reed sticks. Don’t mistake that for a reason to start again.
>
> I’m taking the college place.
>
> I’d like you to come with me.
>
> We can discuss the practicalities after you stop looking surprised.
>
> Owen

Mara handed it back.

Ellis folded it carefully along its original creases.

“I thought,” he said, then stopped.

Mara waited.

“I thought he had already chosen.”

“He had.”

Ellis closed his eyes.

Downstairs, through the open doorway behind Mara, Ruth called her name.

Mara did not turn.

“We repaired it,” she said.

Ellis looked up.

“The kite.”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“Several people.”

“Is it—”

“It’s repaired. That’s all I can promise.”

A smile passed across his face and vanished.

“I have work.”

“Yes.”

“There are things here that need—”

“Yes.”

He looked at the shelves.

Mara recognized the look. She had worn it at the foot of her ladder the day before.

On the counter lay his paper kite, still unfinished.

Beside it, the ledger stood open.

“What happens if you leave?” she asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Have you tried?”

“No.”

From downstairs came the sound of Ruth climbing the stairs.

Ellis looked past Mara.

Ruth appeared in the bedroom doorway, one hand still on the rail.

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then Ruth said, “You look dreadful.”

Ellis laughed.

It was an awkward, startled sound.

“You’ve changed your hair.”

“Thirty-eight years, Ellis. I’ve changed several things.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Yes,” Ruth said. “You can start with that.”

He looked down at the letter.

Then at Mara.

Then at the blue door.

“Do we have wind?”

Ruth wiped her face impatiently.

“We’re on the coast, you fool.”

---

They took the kite to the headland.

Peter carried the reel.

Ruth carried the letter in a waterproof envelope because, she said, she had not waited nearly four decades to lose it to a puddle.

Mara carried the kite.

Ellis walked beside her, blinking often, as though the daylight required adjustment.

Behind them, the village assembled without anyone admitting to organizing it.

The children came first.

Then Ludo.

Then the woman who had supplied the wood.

The dog arrived last and appeared willing to reconsider its earlier assessment.

At the top of the path, the listening house stood against a sky full of moving light.

The wind was steady.

Ellis examined the frame.

His hands remembered what to do before the rest of him seemed convinced.

He checked the knots.

Adjusted a wire.

Freed the high reed with the tip of a borrowed penknife.

Mara watched him.

“Was it broken?”

“No,” he said. “Just stuck.”

Ruth gave him a look.

“Don’t.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were about to.”

Peter handed him the reel.

They carried the kite into the grass.

The first attempt failed.

It rose a little, tipped, and settled nose-first into a clump of weeds.

One of the children clapped anyway.

The second attempt flew for three seconds.

On the third, a gust caught the sail.

The kite pulled sharply upward.

Ellis stumbled.

Mara caught his elbow.

The reel turned.

Above them, the great pale wings found the wind.

At first there was only a thin whistle.

Then a lower note joined it.

Then another.

The sound was strange and uneven, changing with each movement of the frame. Not music in the ordinary sense. Not speech. A chord opened, frayed, and gathered itself again.

Below the cliff, water moved through the rocks.

Mara listened.

Wind through reeds.

Wind through stone.

Water arriving repeatedly.

Her father had not been wrong about those things.

Neither had she.

Beside her, Ellis held the line with both hands.

Ruth stood close enough that their shoulders touched.

No one said Owen’s name.

No one had to.

Far overhead, the kite shifted in the light, and for a moment its rough, repaired wings cast a shadow over all of them.

---

Mara missed the noon train.

This was not an accident.

She missed the next one because Ruth insisted on lunch.

The one after that left while Peter was explaining, at considerable length, why lemon cake was not a serious solution to unhappiness.

By the time she reached the station, the sky had begun to turn the soft blue-gray of early evening.

Ellis walked with her.

He had decided to stay in Bellwether for a few days.

“Can the department manage?”

“Apparently.”

“Apparently?”

“When I went back for my coat, there was someone at the counter.”

“Who?”

“A woman I’d never seen before.”

“What did she say?”

“She told me I was blocking the deliveries.”

Mara smiled.

He handed her a small paper bag.

Inside was her five-pound deposit and a new cassette player, still in its box.

“I returned the old one.”

“I know.”

“Then what’s this?”

“A practical arrangement.”

“Is it from the department?”

“No. Ruth’s shop.”

“You bought it?”

“She gave me a discount.”

“That sounds unlike her.”

“She added it back on for emotional inconvenience.”

The train lights appeared down the track.

Mara held the paper bag against her coat.

“Will I see the door again?”

Ellis considered.

“Probably.”

“That’s not reassuring.”

“It isn’t meant to be.”

The train slowed beside them.

Mara stepped aboard, then turned.

“Was the crate really heavier before I came?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Ellis glanced toward the sea.

“I think,” he said, “because you were carrying it from farther away.”

The doors began to close.

Mara nearly told him that this was exactly the sort of answer that made people distrust departments.

Instead, she raised her hand.

He raised his.

The train moved.

For a while, she watched the platform recede. Then the village vanished behind the curve of the coast.

She opened the cassette player.

Fitted the batteries.

Inserted the tape.

Outside, the evening fields stretched toward the darkening hills.

“All right,” her father said. “The red one.”

Mara leaned her head against the window.

“Obvious in retrospect,” she said with him.

---

When she returned to her building, the yellow wall was dry.

The blue door was gone.

In its place hung a small brass hook.

On the hook was a key.

Not the key to the listening house. She had left that with Ruth.

This one was smaller, silver, with a paper label tied to it.

**Mr. Vale, upstairs.**

Mara looked toward the staircase.

She had lived beneath Mr. Vale for six years.

She knew that he received three newspapers on Saturdays, that he watered his plants at precisely seven in the morning, and that he had once worked in a profession requiring an unreasonable number of rulers.

She did not know why he had stopped leaving his apartment.

She had meant to ask.

Mara set down her bag.

Then she climbed the stairs.

At his door, she hesitated.

Inside, a radio murmured.

She raised her hand and knocked twice, paused, then knocked once.

The radio went quiet.

After a moment, Mr. Vale said, “Who is it?”

“Mara. From downstairs.”

A chair scraped.

“What’s wrong?”

“Nothing.”

“That would be a first.”

She looked at the key in her hand.

“I was wondering,” she said, “whether you might like some tea.”

There was a long silence.

Then footsteps.

Then the sound of a lock.

---

Mr. Vale opened the door exactly as far as the chain allowed.

He wore a shirt buttoned to the throat and a cardigan with a missing pocket. His white hair stood up on one side, and he held a ruler in his left hand.

Mara found this reassuring.

“Tea?” he said.

“Yes.”

“It’s half past nine.”

“I know.”

“People don’t generally offer tea at half past nine.”

“I’ve had an unusual day.”

He considered her shoes.

“Those yours?”

“Borrowed.”

“From someone with sense.”

“So I’ve been told.”

His gaze dropped to the key.

The ruler lowered.

“Where did you get that?”

“Downstairs.”

“Downstairs where?”

“On a hook.”

“There isn’t a hook.”

“There is now.”

He stared at her for another moment.

Then he closed the door.

Mara stood very still.

The chain slid.

When the door opened again, he stepped aside.

“Well,” he said, “you’d better come in before this becomes a conversation in a corridor.”

The apartment smelled of tea, paper, and furniture polish.

There were books everywhere, but not in the casual abundance Mara knew from her own rooms. These were organized into short, exact rows with slips of card marking divisions. Maps covered the walls. Small pencil marks crowded their margins.

On the dining table stood a model railway.

Not a toy.

A town.

Buildings made from painted card lined narrow streets. Trees rose from green fields. A river curved beneath three bridges, each constructed with extraordinary care.

The track ran through the center, around a hill, and stopped at the edge of an unfinished station.

Mara stepped closer.

Mr. Vale watched her.

“If you say charming, I shall ask you to leave.”

“I was going to say detailed.”

“You may remain.”

She bent to look at the station.

Its platform was complete. Its roof was not.

A tiny clock stood above the ticket office.

The hands pointed to 12:16.

Mara straightened.

“Where is this?”

“It isn’t anywhere.”

“It looks like somewhere.”

“That’s generally the objective.”

He shuffled toward the kitchen.

“How do you take it?”

“Milk. No sugar.”

“Good.”

The kettle clicked on.

Mara looked at the model again.

A little blue door stood in the side of the station.

There was no brass plate.

But there was, she noticed, a fern in the window.

“Mr. Vale?”

“Arthur.”

“Arthur. Have you ever heard of the Department of Unfinished Business?”

The kettle hissed.

In the kitchen, something ceramic touched the counter with a small, deliberate sound.

“Yes,” he said.

Mara waited.

Arthur returned carrying two mugs.

“I used to draw their maps.”

---

He said this as though he had once worked for the water board.

Mara accepted her tea.

“The department has maps?”

“Of course it has maps. How else do you suppose all those doors get where they’re going?”

“I thought they found their own way.”

“Trains do that too, if you ignore everyone who laid the track.”

He sat in an armchair beside the model.

Mara took the chair opposite.

“What sort of maps?”

“Probabilities, mostly. Adjacencies. Things close together that don’t share a wall.”

“That doesn’t sound like geography.”

“Much of geography is people insisting that distance should be measured in only one way.”

He pointed to a map above the mantel.

At first Mara saw ordinary streets. Then she realized that the lines between them did not represent roads.

One street was connected to a hospital by a dotted red curve.

A bakery had been linked to a school by something resembling a railway.

Across the corner, in neat ink, was written:

**NORTH DISTRICT: THINGS PEOPLE CANNOT YET SAY.**

She set down her mug.

“That’s real.”

“Yes.”

“All of it?”

“No. Maps aren’t the territory, even when the territory is difficult to explain.”

Mara looked at the silver key.

“What does this open?”

Arthur did not answer immediately.

Then he held out his hand.

She gave it to him.

He turned it over once.

“A drawer.”

“That’s a relief.”

“I wouldn’t be too quick about that.”

He stood, crossed to a desk beneath the window, and unlocked the bottom drawer.

Inside lay a pair of red gloves, a railway timetable, and an unopened envelope.

Mara remained in her chair.

“Another letter?”

“Not exactly.”

Arthur lifted the envelope.

Across the front was a single word.

**COME.**

He stared at it.

Then he laughed bitterly.

“She never did waste paper.”

“Who?”

“My wife.”

Mara looked toward the photographs on the shelf.

There was only one: Arthur, much younger, standing beside a woman in a red coat. They were outdoors in a place of wide sky and low hills. The woman was looking away from the camera, laughing at something beyond the frame.

“Is she—”

“No,” Arthur said. “She’s in Norwich.”

Mara paused.

“Oh.”

“Yes. Norwich. A perfectly survivable distance. People go there every day.”

“Why did she leave?”

“Because I stayed.”

“At the department?”

“At everything.”

He sat again.

For a while, he looked at the unfinished station.

“I retired in February,” he said. “She suggested we travel.”

“And you didn’t want to?”

“I wanted to finish the model.”

Mara waited.

“And before that, the district maps,” he continued. “Before that, a survey. Before that, something else. There was always a legitimate reason.”

He put the envelope on the table.

“Eventually she said she would go first.”

“The letter?”

“Arrived in March.”

“You haven’t opened it.”

“I know what it says.”

“You know one word.”

“Sometimes one word is quite enough.”

Mara thought of Owen’s invitation.

Of Ellis behind the counter.

Of her own wall, finished at last.

“Would you like me to leave?”

Arthur looked surprised.

“Why?”

“So you can open it.”

He rubbed his thumb across the envelope.

Then he shook his head.

“No,” he said. “I think I’d prefer you stayed.”

---

Inside was a photograph.

Arthur’s wife sat on a bench outside a small house with green windows. Beside her was an empty chair.

On the back, she had written:

> Arthur,
>
> The garden is too large, the kitchen window sticks, and there is a railway bridge nearby you would complain about and then admire.
>
> I miss you.
>
> That is not the same as agreeing to wait forever.
>
> Come on Thursday if you’re coming. I’ll make something.
>
> Elspeth

Arthur read it twice.

“What day is it?” he asked.

“Tuesday.”

He closed his eyes.

“Tuesday,” he repeated.

Mara glanced at the model.

“Could someone finish that while you’re away?”

“No.”

“Could it stay unfinished?”

He opened his eyes.

The question seemed to trouble him more than anything else she had said.

He looked at the station roof, the tools arranged beside it, the tiny waiting figures standing on the platform.

“Yes,” he said at last.

He sounded surprised.

“Yes, I suppose it could.”

---

They spent the next hour making arrangements.

Not grand ones.

Mara found the current train timetable.

Arthur made a list of what he needed to pack.

Then he made another list to correct the first.

Then Mara took the pencil away.

At ten past eleven, he telephoned Elspeth.

Mara went into the kitchen.

She could still hear his voice, though not the words.

At first it was careful.

Then defensive.

Then quiet.

At one point, he laughed.

Mara washed the mugs and left them upside down beside the sink.

When she returned, Arthur was standing by the photograph.

“Thursday,” he said.

“Good.”

“She says I should bring the blue coat.”

“Will you?”

“I dislike being predictable.”

“Bring the coat.”

He nodded.

At the door, he handed her the silver key.

“I don’t need this anymore.”

Mara almost took it.

Then she shook her head.

“Keep it.”

“What for?”

She considered the apartment, the model town, the maps of difficult distances.

“In case you come back.”

Arthur looked down at the key.

Then he slipped it into his pocket.

“Tea at half past nine,” he said. “Extraordinary behavior.”

“I’ll try not to make a habit of it.”

“Don’t be hasty.”

---

Downstairs, the brass hook was empty.

Mara stood before it for a moment.

Then she went into her apartment, took off the borrowed shoes, and set them neatly by the door to be returned.

Her rooms were exactly as she had left them.

A book lay open facedown on the sofa.

Three unpaid bills waited beneath a bowl.

The plant on the windowsill leaned toward the glass with the weary determination of something negotiating a long-term disappointment.

Mara watered it.

She put the bills where she would see them.

She marked her place in the book.

None of this changed her life.

Not all at once.

But the room seemed easier to stand in afterward.

She called her mother.

It was late.

Her mother answered on the sixth ring.

“Mara?”

“Hi.”

“What’s happened?”

“Nothing bad.”

There was a pause.

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

“All right.”

Mara sat on the kitchen floor because it was the nearest place to sit.

“I went to the sea.”

“At this time of year?”

“Yes.”

“Were you wearing those boots?”

Mara laughed.

Her mother waited.

“I borrowed shoes.”

“Good.”

Outside, a bus sighed at the stop.

Inside, the refrigerator hummed.

“I found something of Dad’s,” Mara said.

The silence on the line changed.

“What?”

“A tape.”

“Oh.”

“Did you know about Bellwether?”

After a moment, her mother said, “Some of it.”

“Could you tell me?”

“Yes.”

“Not everything tonight.”

“No.”

“Just something.”

Her mother took a breath.

“When he came back from that summer,” she said, “he had a terrible haircut and believed he could make soup.”

Mara leaned her head against the cupboard.

“He could make soup.”

“Not then.”

And her mother began.

---

On Thursday morning, Mara carried Arthur’s suitcase down the stairs.

It was heavier than his length of stay seemed to justify.

“Tools?” she asked.

“Some.”

“How many?”

“There’s a window.”

At the front door, he stopped to button his blue coat.

The yellow wall caught the morning light.

The brass hook had vanished.

In its place was a small rectangle of unpainted plaster.

Mara stared.

Arthur followed her gaze.

“Departments,” he said.

She laughed.

Then they walked to the station.

---

After Arthur’s train left, Mara had intended to go directly to work.

She had a ceiling to prepare on the other side of town and a client who believed the phrase *a quick refresh* could reduce the physical time required for plaster to dry.

Instead, she found herself in front of a bookshop.

In the window stood a hand-painted sign.

**WANTED: SOMEONE TO FINISH THE MURAL IN THE CHILDREN’S ROOM.  
PREVIOUS ARTIST RAN AWAY TO BECOME AN ACCOUNTANT.**

Mara read it.

The door opened.

A woman in paint-splashed trousers leaned out.

“You paint?”

“Walls.”

“That’s what it’s on.”

“I mean houses.”

“We’re trying not to be prejudiced.”

Mara looked past her.

At the back of the shop, visible through a low arch, an unfinished forest spread across a wall. Half a fox watched from a bramble. A river stopped abruptly where the pencil sketch began.

“I have work.”

“Most people do. That’s why I put the sign in the window instead of waiting for destiny.”

Mara smiled despite herself.

“What’s your name?”

“Imogen.”

“Mara.”

Imogen opened the door a little wider.

“Five minutes?”

Mara thought of the ceiling.

Of the drying plaster.

Of all the things that took the time they took, regardless of what anyone called them.

“Five,” she said.

The shop smelled of paper and coffee.

Somewhere in the back, a child was explaining to an adult that whales did not need eyebrows.

Imogen led her beneath the arch.

The mural was worse up close.

The perspective wandered.

The fox’s finished half looked judgmental.

The river flowed uphill.

Mara folded her arms.

“This will take more than five minutes.”

“I suspected as much.”

“Do you have the original sketches?”

“A folder full.”

“A ladder?”

“Two. One is trustworthy.”

“What’s the budget?”

Imogen told her.

Mara looked at the mural again.

“That’s not terrible.”

“We’ve been saving.”

There was a little desk beneath the window. On it lay a sketchbook, several pencils, and a postcard showing the coast at Bellwether.

Mara picked up the postcard.

On the back, in Ruth’s handwriting, was a brief message.

**He’s here. The kite works.  
Tell your sister we’ll need more wood.**

Mara turned.

“Your sister?”

“The woman with the ash strips,” Imogen said. “I wondered when you’d notice.”

Mara put the postcard down very slowly.

“Does everyone know everyone?”

“No,” said Imogen. “But the interesting mistakes do tend to travel.”

---

She took the job.

Not immediately.

She measured the wall, photographed the sketches, and went to finish the ceiling she had already promised to finish.

That afternoon, between coats, she drew a fox in the margin of an invoice.

It was not a good fox.

It looked like a dog who had heard something disappointing.

She drew another.

This one was worse.

The third had possibilities.

By evening, there were seven foxes across the bottom of the page.

Her client looked at them.

“Are those included?”

“Only if you want them on the ceiling.”

He considered this longer than she expected.

“Maybe in the small bedroom.”

Mara laughed.

Then realized he was serious.

“All right,” she said. “We can talk about it.”

---

The department did not appear again for three weeks.

By then, Arthur had sent a postcard from Norwich.

On the front was a railway bridge.

On the back, he had written:

**Poor drainage. Elegant span.  
Elspeth sends regards.**

Ruth sent a parcel containing Mara’s boots, which she had somehow left under the counter, and a jar of honey wrapped in newspaper.

Ellis included a note.

**The house will take years.  
This is not a complaint.**

Mara pinned both messages above her desk.

The mural progressed.

The fox acquired its missing half.

The river was persuaded downhill.

Imogen declared the trees less smug than the previous artist’s trees, a judgment Mara accepted without attempting to understand.

On a Friday evening, when the bookshop had closed and rain moved down the front window in crooked lines, Mara climbed off the ladder to clean her brushes.

A blue door stood beneath the painted oak.

It was much smaller than before.

Child-sized.

Mara looked at it.

“No,” she said.

A voice from the other side replied, “We’ve been told to expect that.”

It was not Ellis.

Mara set down the brush jar.

“Who are you?”

“Evening deliveries.”

“I’m working.”

“So are we.”

The latch lifted.

A small girl stepped through carrying a cardboard box tied with green string.

She was perhaps ten. She wore a school uniform, a yellow raincoat, and the expression of a person deeply unimpressed by the organizational abilities of adults.

Mara looked from her to the doorway.

“Do your parents know you’re here?”

“My mother’s at the counter.”

“Good.”

“She says if anybody asks, this is work experience.”

“Is it?”

“No. I’m helping.”

The girl held out the box.

“This is yours.”

Mara did not take it.

“What is it?”

“I’m not allowed to say.”

“Do you know?”

“Yes.”

“That seems inefficient.”

“We’re a department.”

Mara sighed.

“Of course you are.”

She accepted the box.

It was light.

On the lid, in her own handwriting, was her name.

Not her handwriting now.

Her handwriting at twelve, when she dotted her *i*s with stars and believed adulthood would arrive accompanied by a clear set of instructions.

The girl looked at the mural.

“The fox needs a secret.”

“What?”

“A secret. Otherwise he’s just standing there.”

Mara looked at the fox.

The girl was right.

“Any suggestions?”

“He knows where the river goes.”

“It goes to the sea.”

“Does it?”

Before Mara could answer, the girl stepped backward through the little door.

“Returns before closing,” she said.

Then the door shut.

Mara stood in the painted forest holding the box.

After a while, she untied the string.

Inside lay a cheap red notebook.

She recognized it at once.

She had thought it lost in one of the moves after her father died.

On the first page was a title written in enormous letters.

**PLACES I WILL GO WHEN I CAN GO ANYWHERE**

Below it, in smaller writing:

**By Mara Finch, who will not be boring.**

She sat down on the floor.

The list was ambitious.

There were real places, misspelled places, and several places that did not exist.

A city built on bridges.

A library where books grew back after being burned.

A mountain you could climb only by telling the truth.

A house with a different sea outside every window.

In the margins, she had drawn maps.

Terrible maps.

Enthusiastic maps.

Maps of distances she had never expected to measure in years.

Halfway through the notebook, the lists stopped.

The remaining pages were blank.

Mara turned one.

Then another.

Outside the children’s room, Imogen was counting the till.

“Everything all right?”

Mara looked at the notebook.

“Yes,” she said.

“What have you found?”

She thought about the crate.

The tape.

The key.

The letter with the empty chair.

Then she picked up a pencil.

“Somewhere to begin,” she said.

---

She did not start with the mountain.

That seemed demanding for a Friday evening.

Instead, she drew the bookshop.

She drew the painted oak, the fox, the little blue door, and the river that had finally agreed to flow in the proper direction.

Beyond the edge of the mural, where there was not enough wall for anything else, she drew a small harbor.

Then a path.

Then a listening house above the sea.

Imogen appeared in the archway.

“Is that for the mural?”

“Maybe.”

“Can we afford it?”

“It’s only a drawing.”

“That is how things begin to get expensive.”

Mara smiled.

Imogen sat beside her.

For a few minutes, neither spoke.

Then Imogen pointed to a blank corner of the page.

“What goes there?”

“I don’t know.”

“Good.”

“Why good?”

“Room.”

Mara looked at the blank space.

Then at the wall.

The fox seemed to be watching them.

She sharpened her pencil.

---

By the following week, children had begun asking where the river went.

Mara had planned to leave it open.

This proved optimistic.

“It can’t just go off the wall,” said a boy named Sami.

“It can,” said Imogen. “Many things do.”

“Then it should go somewhere.”

A girl beside him, who had introduced herself as Bee and refused to elaborate, pointed toward the narrow strip of plaster above the bookcase.

“There.”

“There’s hardly any space,” Mara said.

“Rivers are thin.”

“Not all rivers.”

“This one is.”

Sami folded his arms.

“The fox knows.”

Mara looked at Imogen.

Imogen looked innocent in a way that immediately disqualified her.

“Have you been telling them things?”

“I own a bookshop.”

Together, they moved the bookcase.

Behind it was an old door.

Not blue.

Not magical.

Just an ordinary wooden door, painted the same cream as the wall, with a keyhole full of dust.

Imogen frowned.

“I’d forgotten that was there.”

“What’s behind it?”

“The old storeroom.”

“Why don’t you use it?”

“Because it’s full.”

“Of what?”

“Things that made it difficult to use as a storeroom.”

Mara gave her a look.

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It has been a very effective one for six years.”

The children watched with immediate interest.

Imogen found the key in a tin beneath the counter.

The lock resisted, then turned.

Inside were boxes, a broken display stand, rolled posters, three mismatched chairs, and a tall mirror wrapped in a sheet.

There was also a window.

A real window, overlooking a small neglected courtyard.

Late light fell across the dusty floor.

Mara stepped inside.

Imogen stayed in the doorway.

“Oh,” she said.

“What?”

“I’d forgotten the window.”

Outside, weeds had grown between the paving stones. A cracked basin stood against the back wall. Above it, a vine climbed toward a rectangle of sky.

Sami peered around Mara.

“The river could come in here.”

Bee nodded.

“And then outside.”

Mara looked back at Imogen.

Imogen sighed.

“How expensive is a river?”

---

It began, as many impractical projects did, with cleaning.

The children were assigned posters.

Imogen carried boxes.

Mara opened the window and discovered that it stuck because someone had painted the sash shut.

She freed it with a blade.

The courtyard air entered, damp and cool.

One of the chairs had a broken leg.

The mirror was intact.

The posters dated from a succession of long-forgotten readings, except for one advertising an author who had become famous enough that Imogen held it up and said something unsuitable in front of the children.

By six, the room had a floor again.

By seven, they had found an old sink beneath a stack of promotional cardboard castles.

By eight, Mara understood that the river had become a larger undertaking than anyone intended.

She was tired.

Her hands hurt.

There was dust in her hair.

She had a dinner appointment with her mother at eight-thirty.

For a moment, the old impulse rose: stay until it was done, solve it now, make the project complete before allowing anything else to happen.

Then she looked at the unfinished room.

“It can wait until Monday,” she said.

Imogen looked up from a box.

“Yes,” she said. “It can.”

Mara washed her hands.

She left the brushes clean, the window shut, and the work unfinished.

Outside, the evening city moved around her.

She was on time for dinner.

---

Her mother had brought a photograph album.

“I found it in the top cupboard.”

Mara set down her coat.

“The one you said was too high to reach?”

“I used a chair.”

“Mum.”

“A sturdy chair.”

“Still.”

Her mother waved this away with the practiced efficiency of someone who had spent decades refusing reasonable criticism from people she remembered as toddlers.

The restaurant was small and warm. Rain tapped against the awning outside.

They ordered.

Then her mother opened the album.

There was Mara at three, furious beside a birthday cake.

Mara at six, asleep inside a laundry basket.

Her father holding a fish so small it seemed an insult to both fisherman and fish.

Her mother on a bicycle, laughing.

Mara touched the edge of the photograph.

“Where was this?”

“Before you.”

“A lot seems to have happened before me.”

“We were unexpectedly busy.”

They turned pages.

At the back was a photograph of Bellwether.

The listening house stood above the bay.

Her father leaned against the gate.

Ellis sat in the grass.

Ruth was looking directly into the camera.

Owen, half out of frame, held one wing of the kite.

Mara stared.

“You had this?”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you show me?”

Her mother’s face changed.

“I thought it would upset you.”

“It would have.”

“I know.”

“That doesn’t mean—”

“I know,” her mother said again.

They sat quietly.

The waiter brought bread.

Neither reached for it.

At last Mara said, “I think we’ve been trying not to upset each other for a very long time.”

Her mother looked down at the photograph.

“Yes.”

“It hasn’t really worked.”

“No.”

Mara took a piece of bread.

Her mother did too.

Outside, the rain became heavier.

Inside, they began, awkwardly and without any map at all, to tell each other the things they had been carefully leaving out.

---

The courtyard became a reading garden.

Not quickly.

Not because a mysterious door appeared with a parcel containing everything they needed.

They needed permits.

They needed drainage.

They needed a person who understood why water collected against the back wall.

Arthur knew such a person.

Unfortunately, that person was Arthur.

He returned from Norwich with Elspeth, two suitcases, and a long cardboard tube full of drawings.

“I said a drain,” Mara told him.

“I heard you.”

“This is a plan for the whole courtyard.”

“A drain exists in a context.”

Elspeth kissed Mara on both cheeks.

“He’s been impossible. I thought I’d bring him somewhere useful.”

She was taller than Mara expected, with silver hair cut short and a red scarf looped twice around her neck.

“Are you staying?”

“For a week.”

Arthur unrolled the drawings.

“Possibly ten days.”

Elspeth looked at him.

“A week.”

He adjusted a corner of the paper.

“A productive week.”

They built raised beds from reclaimed timber.

Peter came from Bellwether and declared the available tea inadequate.

Ruth sent plants.

Ellis sent a small wind instrument made from copper and wood, with instructions that were unusually clear until the final sentence:

**Let it be imperfect enough to belong outdoors.**

Mara hung it above the basin.

On the wall, she painted the river.

It flowed from the children’s room, through the old storeroom, beneath a window, and out into the courtyard, where its painted water met the real shadow of a vine.

The fox followed along one edge.

He had a secret now.

Mara never decided precisely what it was.

That seemed fair.

---

On the morning they opened the garden, a woman arrived with a young girl in a yellow raincoat.

Mara recognized the girl first.

“Evening deliveries.”

The girl grinned.

“It’s daytime.”

The woman held out her hand.

“Nadia.”

“You’re at the counter.”

“Usually.”

Mara shook her hand.

“Do you want tea?”

“More than almost anything.”

They sat beneath the vine while the children inspected the shelves.

The girl had brought a book and immediately began reading it upside down.

Mara glanced at her.

“Is that easier?”

“No,” the girl said. “Different.”

Nadia accepted a mug from Imogen.

“I’ve heard good things.”

“About the garden?”

“About the river.”

Mara looked at the painted wall.

“Does it connect somewhere?”

Nadia followed her gaze.

“Everything connects somewhere.”

“That sounds like a department answer.”

“It’s my day off. You should hear the official version.”

They drank their tea.

Above them, the wind instrument sounded a soft, uneven chord.

“Do you enjoy the work?” Mara asked.

Nadia considered.

“Sometimes.”

“And the rest?”

“Sometimes people want us to give them back what they’ve lost.”

“You can’t.”

“No.”

“Sometimes they want a clear answer.”

“We’re not very good at those.”

“What are you good at?”

Nadia watched her daughter turn a page.

“Getting something within reach.”

Mara looked at the open door leading through the storeroom to the shop.

A child ran past carrying a book nearly as large as his chest.

Arthur argued with Peter about whether the basin was level.

Elspeth and Ruth sat together in the sun as if they had known each other for years.

Imogen stood in the doorway, flour on one sleeve, holding a tray of cake.

Mara thought about all the improbable machinery that had begun with a door in a yellow wall.

Then she thought about the ordinary machinery: trains, phone calls, soup, glue, people agreeing to show up.

“Does everyone get a door?” she asked.

“No,” Nadia said.

“Why not?”

“Sometimes they get a person.”

---

That afternoon, Mara found a parcel beneath the painted oak.

No label.

No string.

Just a small cardboard box with a square hole cut in one side.

Inside was a tiny wooden station roof.

She carried it into the courtyard.

Arthur was examining a paving stone.

“Is this yours?”

He looked at the box.

“No.”

“It looks like part of your model.”

“It is.”

“That usually means it’s yours.”

“Not anymore.”

Mara waited.

Arthur stood.

“I’m giving the railway to the shop.”

Imogen, passing with empty cups, stopped.

“You’re what?”

“The town is suitable for children, provided they understand that the west bridge is structurally delicate.”

Imogen put down the cups.

“Arthur, it’s enormous.”

“Yes.”

“We haven’t room.”

He looked toward the cleared storeroom.

Mara laughed.

Imogen narrowed her eyes.

“You’ve planned this.”

Arthur said nothing.

Elspeth, from the bench, said, “He drew three versions.”

Mara looked into the box.

“You finished the roof.”

“Yes.”

“How does that feel?”

Arthur considered the question.

Then he glanced at Elspeth.

“Useful,” he said.

---

Moving the town took all of Sunday.

The hills came apart.

The river lifted in two sections.

The west bridge required six hands, three towels, and absolute silence.

Peter was banned from helping after describing the station as charming.

Imogen cleared a low table.

Mara painted the legs blue.

By evening, the railway stood in the old storeroom beneath the window.

Arthur connected the power.

A small locomotive waited beside the station.

The children gathered.

Sami held his breath so visibly that Bee told him it would not make the train go faster.

Arthur turned the control.

The engine moved.

It passed the completed station, crossed the river, entered the cutting, and disappeared behind a hill.

When it emerged, something was different.

Mara leaned closer.

The little blue door in the station wall stood open.

Inside, scarcely visible, was a counter.

Behind the counter, a tiny figure in a gray cardigan bent over a kite.

Mara looked at Arthur.

He looked at the model.

For a long moment, neither spoke.

Then Arthur switched off the train.

The blue door was painted shut.

The station contained only the ticket office he had built.

Imogen looked from one of them to the other.

“What?”

Arthur picked up a ruler.

“I may,” he said carefully, “have been slightly careless with scale.”

---

They waited until the children had gone home before turning the railway on again.

Imogen locked the front door.

Peter made tea.

Elspeth stayed in the courtyard, explaining that she had spent many years married to a cartographer and had learned when to sit somewhere comfortable.

Arthur crouched beside the table.

Mara watched the station.

The locomotive circled the hill.

At the third pass, the blue door opened.

This time, a tiny figure stepped onto the platform.

Not Ellis.

A woman carrying a clipboard.

She looked up.

Even at that size, her expression was unmistakably irritated.

Arthur closed his eyes.

“Oh, dear.”

“Who is that?” Mara whispered.

“Compliance.”

The woman raised one hand.

A moment later, a full-sized blue door appeared beside the sink.

Imogen stared at it.

“If that blocks the plumbing, I’m charging rent.”

The door opened.

The woman with the clipboard stepped through.

She wore a dark coat, practical shoes, and spectacles on a chain.

“Arthur Vale.”

“Beatrice.”

“Still treating the boundary between representation and infrastructure as a matter of personal taste, I see.”

“It was a model.”

“It is a junction.”

“Only a small one.”

Beatrice looked at the railway.

“Scale,” she said, “has never been an adequate defense.”

---

Mara had expected a reprimand.

There was one.

It lasted fourteen minutes and included the phrases *unregistered adjacency*, *unauthorized miniature access*, and *a regrettable precedent involving a dollhouse in Leeds*.

Imogen took notes.

Not because she intended to comply with anything.

Because, she told Mara afterward, some phrases were too good to waste.

Arthur accepted the lecture with the strained dignity of a retired professional encountering regulations he had once helped write.

Finally, Beatrice closed her folder.

“The junction must either be sealed or assigned a responsible custodian.”

“Seal it,” Arthur said immediately.

Mara looked at him.

He returned her look.

“I have train tickets for Thursday.”

Beatrice nodded once.

“An encouraging development.”

Imogen folded her arms.

“What does custodianship involve?”

Mara turned.

“No.”

“I asked a question.”

“That’s how it starts.”

Beatrice consulted her clipboard.

“Basic maintenance. A clearly marked threshold. No unsupervised access by minors. A log of unusual deliveries. Tea is not formally required but statistically advisable.”

“And what comes through?”

“Mostly misplaced opportunities.”

“Mostly?”

Beatrice looked up.

“We cannot guarantee perfect sorting.”

Imogen glanced around the old storeroom: the train, the painted river, the open doorway to the garden.

Then she looked at Mara.

“This room used to contain cardboard castles.”

“That isn’t an argument.”

“It is to me.”

Mara sighed.

Beatrice held out a form.

Imogen read it carefully.

For the first time, Beatrice looked impressed.

“You’re reading the conditions.”

“I run a bookshop.”

“You would be surprised how little that predicts.”

Imogen pointed to a clause.

“No.”

Beatrice leaned forward.

“What is your objection?”

“Indefinite availability.”

“It’s standard.”

“So are opening hours.”

There was a pause.

Mara watched Beatrice’s face.

Then, very slowly, Beatrice smiled.

“Go on.”

---

They negotiated until nine.

The final arrangement allowed deliveries between ten and five, Monday through Saturday, excluding lunch, inventory days, and any occasion on which Imogen deemed the circumstances unreasonable.

Beatrice insisted that unreasonable needed definition.

Imogen said she would know it when she saw it.

Arthur suggested a schedule.

Elspeth came in from the courtyard and suggested everyone stop being ridiculous.

They compromised.

Mara refused formal responsibility for the railway.

She agreed to repaint the threshold.

Peter offered to provide cake at a rate everyone considered excessive except Peter.

At last, Beatrice signed.

Imogen signed.

Arthur witnessed, after adding a note that the junction’s dimensions might fluctuate during electrical faults.

Beatrice read the note twice.

“I do wish you’d mentioned that earlier.”

“You were lecturing.”

“That has never stopped you before.”

She gathered her papers.

At the door, she turned to Mara.

“You opened a crate at the north district branch.”

“Yes.”

“How was the service?”

Mara blinked.

“Unexpected.”

Beatrice wrote something down.

“Common response.”

“Ellis was kind.”

The pencil paused.

Then Beatrice nodded.

“I’ll note that separately.”

She stepped through the blue door.

It closed.

The sink was visible again.

On the wall above it hung a small brass plate.

**BOOKSHOP ANNEX**  
**Please observe local opening hours.**  
**A delivery is not a command.**

Mara read the last line.

Then she looked at Imogen.

“I like that bit.”

“So do I,” Imogen said.

---

The first delivery arrived on a Tuesday.

It was a tin of buttons.

Imogen found it on the station platform, where it was much too large to have fitted.

Attached was a label.

**FOR THE MAN WHO ALWAYS ASKS FOR THE SAME BOOK.**

“That narrows it down badly,” Imogen said.

Mara was painting a small boat in the corner of the mural.

“What book?”

“There are several men. Several books. People are less various than publishers hope.”

They put the tin behind the counter.

At eleven, a young man came in and asked whether they had a certain guide to repairing old radios.

They did not.

He looked relieved.

Mara noticed.

Imogen noticed Mara noticing.

“Would you like us to order it?” Imogen asked.

“No, that’s all right. I’ll try again.”

“You’ve tried again four times.”

“Have I?”

“Yes.”

He adjusted the strap of his bag.

His gaze moved toward the door.

Imogen reached beneath the counter.

“I wonder if this might be yours.”

She set down the tin.

The young man went pale.

“My grandmother had one like that.”

Mara put down her brush.

The man touched the lid.

It was decorated with faded roses.

“She kept buttons in it,” he said.

“We had a suspicion.”

He opened it.

Inside were hundreds of buttons.

Black coat buttons.

Pearl shirt buttons.

A green one shaped like a leaf.

Beneath them lay a folded piece of paper.

He did not touch the paper.

“I don’t understand.”

Imogen leaned lightly against the counter.

“Neither do we, most days.”

He looked up.

“Is this a joke?”

“No.”

“Who gave you this?”

“A delivery.”

“From where?”

Mara thought of all the possible answers.

Then she said, “Somewhere that thought you might be ready to have it back.”

The man shut the lid.

“I’m not.”

“All right,” Imogen said.

He stared at her.

“All right?”

“You can leave it here.”

“For how long?”

She glanced at the brass plate visible through the arch.

“As long as we reasonably can.”

The young man stood still for another moment.

Then he nodded once.

“Thank you.”

He left without asking about the radio book again.

Imogen put the tin on a shelf beneath the counter.

Mara picked up her brush.

They did not discuss whether he would return.

---

He came back on Friday.

This time he bought a book about birds.

His name was Jonah.

He told them this while studying the receipt.

“My grandmother taught me their names.”

Imogen nodded.

“She had a radio,” he continued. “It stopped working after she died. I thought if I fixed it—”

He stopped.

Mara waited.

“I don’t know what I thought.”

“Would you like the tin?”

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

He touched the bird book.

“Can I sit in the garden?”

“Of course.”

He stayed for an hour.

When he left, the book was full of slips of paper marking pages.

The next week, he brought a screwdriver and repaired the loose hinge on the courtyard gate.

No one had asked him to.

The week after that, he took the tin home.

He did not open it in the shop.

Mara never learned what the note said.

She was beginning to understand that not knowing was sometimes part of doing the work properly.

---

Summer arrived.

The vine climbed.

The children’s room acquired cushions.

The railway developed a fault that caused the locomotive to stop at the bridge every time someone in the shop said the word *eventually*.

Arthur blamed the wiring.

Beatrice, consulted by post, blamed Arthur.

Imogen banned the word until he could visit.

Mara continued painting houses.

Sometimes she painted foxes in small bedrooms.

Sometimes she drew maps in the red notebook after dinner.

She went to Bellwether with her mother.

They took the early train.

Her mother brought sandwiches, though Ruth had promised lunch.

“People can promise anything,” she said. “It’s sensible to have sandwiches.”

At the listening house, scaffolding rose along one wall.

Ellis stood on a ladder, arguing with a carpenter about the placement of an air channel.

Ruth waited by the gate.

Mara’s mother stopped when she saw her.

For a moment, the years between them seemed visible.

Then Ruth opened her arms.

Mara looked away toward the sea.

The kite flew above the headland.

Not always.

Only when the wind was right and someone had time to take it out.

That, she thought, was better than having it fixed forever in the sky.

---

They stayed two nights.

On the second evening, Mara walked alone to the bay.

The tide was low.

She followed the rocks until she found a place where water moved through a narrow passage in the stone.

The sound was whispering.

Undeniably.

Not words, exactly.

Not her name.

But enough like language that the mind leaned toward it.

She sat on a dry rock and listened.

For years, she had thought being wrong would mean losing the argument.

Now she understood that there were arguments nobody had been trying to win.

She took the cassette player from her bag.

The plastic had already acquired a scratch.

She pressed play.

Her father’s voice arrived through the small speaker, thin against the sea but unmistakable.

“I wanted us to have an expedition.”

“We did,” Mara said.

The water shifted.

The tape hissed.

Above her, somewhere beyond the cliff edge, the kite found a new chord.

She stayed until the light changed.

Then she put the player away and climbed back toward the path, where her mother was waiting with a sandwich wrapped in paper.

---

In August, the bookshop annex received a parcel addressed to no one.

It was heavy.

Not metaphorically.

Mara and Imogen had to lift it together.

Inside was a stone.

Smooth, gray, roughly the size of a loaf of bread.

No inscription.

No note.

Imogen stared at it.

“I have concerns about the sorting.”

Mara turned the stone over.

On its underside was a faint line of blue paint.

They asked Beatrice.

Her reply arrived two days later.

**Not all items can be identified at point of receipt.  
Please do not throw it away, use it as a doorstop, or attempt to complete it.**

Imogen read the note aloud.

“Complete it?”

Mara looked at the stone.

“How would we do that?”

They put it on a shelf in the garden.

Children touched it.

Adults overlooked it.

Rain darkened it.

Sun warmed it.

By September, a small green shoot had appeared in a crack neither woman remembered seeing.

“It’s a seed?” Imogen asked.

“No idea.”

They watered it cautiously.

The shoot became a plant with narrow silver leaves.

Arthur said it was not on any map.

Ruth said that was not how plants were usually identified.

Nadia visited and looked at it for a long time.

“Do you know what it is?” Mara asked.

“Possibly.”

“Will you tell us?”

“When I’m sure.”

The girl in the yellow raincoat, whose name was Ada, crouched beside the shelf.

“It’s waiting,” she said.

“For what?”

Ada shrugged.

“Better weather, maybe. People always assume it’s something dramatic.”

---

In October, the plant flowered.

One small blue flower.

It opened at closing time.

Imogen noticed first.

“Mara.”

The petals were almost transparent.

In the center lay a tiny brass bell.

No stem attached it.

No thread held it in place.

Mara lifted it carefully.

The flower folded shut.

The bell made no sound when she moved it.

On its rim, in lettering finer than she could have painted, were the words:

**RING ONLY WHEN YOU ARE PREPARED TO BE ANSWERED.**

Imogen read them over her shoulder.

“That seems a sensible general rule.”

“Answered by whom?”

“Presumably the dramatic part.”

They set the bell beneath a glass on the counter.

They did not ring it.

A week passed.

Then another.

It became one of the ordinary peculiarities of the shop, along with the railway’s aversion to *eventually* and the customer who claimed to remember books that had never been written.

Mara found herself looking at the bell when she was tired.

When the invoices piled up.

When the rain made the garden unusable.

When she missed her father with the dull, unremarkable ache that no journey had removed.

She did not ring it then.

Not because she had become wise.

Because she was learning to distinguish wanting an answer from wanting the question to stop.

---

The night she finally rang it, there was a storm.

Not a remarkable storm.

No record-breaking winds, no flooded streets, no sky split open by theatrical lightning.

Just hard rain, a rattling sign, and the kind of cold that made every window seem slightly inadequate.

Imogen had gone home.

Mara was alone, finishing the last stretch of mural above the storeroom door.

She had painted a bridge.

On the bridge stood a small figure with a bag over one shoulder.

She had not decided where the figure was going.

Her phone lay faceup on the table.

There was a message from Ruth.

**The house opened today.  
Come when you can.**

Below it was a photograph.

The listening house glowed in the dusk.

People stood outside beneath umbrellas.

Ellis was laughing at something beyond the frame.

Mara smiled.

Then she set the phone down and looked at the bell.

For the first time, she had no urgent question.

No demand.

No particular hope she was trying to disguise as curiosity.

Only a sense that something had been waiting politely and that she might, at last, have enough room to listen.

She lifted the glass.

Picked up the bell.

And rang it.

The sound was small.

A single clear note.

It lingered much longer than it should have.

The rain continued against the window.

The railway lights came on.

Not all at once.

One station lamp.

Then another.

Then the little houses, street by street.

The locomotive remained still.

Behind the painted bridge, where there had never been a door, a narrow seam appeared.

Mara stepped back.

The seam widened.

Warm light fell across the floor.

A woman stood on the other side.

She wore a long coat with wet shoulders and carried a leather case.

Her hair was dark, streaked with silver.

She looked at Mara with an expression of profound relief.

“Oh, good,” she said. “You’re still open.”

Mara glanced at the clock.

“Technically, no.”

The woman sighed.

“Of course.”

“What do you need?”

The woman looked past her into the shop, toward the painted river, the station, the garden beyond.

“A place to put something down.”

Mara thought of the brass plate.

Of local opening hours.

Of deliveries that were not commands.

Then she looked at the woman’s hands.

They were trembling around the handle of the case.

“For a little while,” Mara said.

The woman nodded.

“That may be enough.”

She stepped inside.

And the rain, for one impossible moment, sounded like applause.

---

The case was ordinary leather, worn pale at the corners.

The woman set it on a chair rather than the floor.

Mara noticed the care she took.

“Tea?”

“Please.”

“Name?”

“Lin.”

“Mara.”

“I know.”

Mara stopped halfway to the kettle.

Lin winced.

“Sorry. That always sounds dreadful. Your name’s on the invoice under the mug.”

Mara looked.

It was.

“Right.”

“I’m not from a department.”

“That’s good to establish early.”

“I used to be.”

“Less reassuring.”

Lin took off her coat.

Beneath it she wore a dark sweater with a repaired cuff.

Mara made the tea.

The shop settled around them, its creaks and small noises made intimate by the rain.

For several minutes, Lin simply held the mug.

Then she looked at the case.

“I was told the bell would find somewhere suitable.”

“Suitable for what?”

“A transfer.”

“Of the case?”

“Of what’s in it.”

Mara sat opposite her.

“I should tell you I’m not in charge here.”

“Who is?”

“Imogen.”

“Can you telephone her?”

“Yes.”

“Will she mind?”

“Probably. That doesn’t mean I shouldn’t.”

She called.

Imogen answered with a toothbrush in her mouth.

Mara explained.

There was a silence.

Then Imogen said, very clearly, “Do not agree to an indefinite arrangement.”

“I haven’t.”

“Do not sign anything.”

“I haven’t.”

“Is she all right?”

Mara looked at Lin.

Lin’s eyes were closed.

“I’m not sure.”

Imogen sighed.

“I’ll be there in ten minutes.”

---

When Imogen arrived, she brought bread, cheese, and a notebook.

“Tea first,” she said. “Terms second.”

Lin opened her eyes.

“Thank you.”

Imogen hung up her coat.

“You’re welcome, provisionally.”

They ate.

Rain ran down the glass.

At last Lin set her cup aside.

“I carried it for twenty-seven years,” she said.

Mara looked at the case.

“What is it?”

Lin unfastened the buckles.

Inside, on dark velvet, lay a clear glass cylinder.

Within the cylinder moved a small light.

Not a flame.

Not electricity.

Something softer, changing shape as they watched.

Mara leaned forward.

The light briefly resembled a doorway.

Then a field.

Then nothing she could name.

Imogen’s pen hovered above her notebook.

“That’s inconveniently difficult to describe.”

“It’s a possibility,” Lin said.

“What kind?”

“One that hasn’t found a place yet.”

Mara thought of the stone.

The waiting flower.

The bell.

“How did you get it?”

“A delivery.”

“And you accepted?”

“I thought I understood.”

“What happened?”

Lin rested her hand beside the cylinder.

“I kept taking it somewhere it could be useful.”

“Was it?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then why—”

“Because useful isn’t the same as right.”

The light turned slowly.

For a moment, Mara saw a harbor with no water.

Then the image dissolved.

Lin continued.

“I tried schools. Hospitals. Stations. Empty buildings. Crowded ones. Places that needed hope. Places that had too much of it and no plan.”

“Twenty-seven years?” Imogen asked.

Lin nodded.

“Did anyone tell you to carry it that long?”

“No.”

The answer was immediate.

It left the room quieter than before.

Mara looked at the repaired cuff.

The careful hands.

The case on the chair.

“You thought putting it down meant failing.”

Lin smiled without amusement.

“Yes.”

Imogen closed her notebook.

“Well,” she said, “we should probably begin by not repeating that.”

---

They moved the cylinder to the center of the table.

Nothing happened.

No hidden choir.

No sudden revelation.

The light continued its small, patient changes.

Lin watched it with the fixed attention of someone who had organized too much of her life around one object.

Mara went to the garden door.

The rain had eased.

Water gathered along the vine.

The basin reflected a dim square of sky.

“Does it need to become something?” she asked.

Lin looked up.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“That’s what possibilities do.”

“Most don’t,” Imogen said.

Lin stared at her.

“Most remain possibilities,” Imogen continued. “I could have been a dentist. Fortunately for everyone, I wasn’t.”

“That’s different.”

“Is it?”

The light brightened.

Very slightly.

Mara returned to the table.

“What happens if no one uses it?”

“I don’t know.”

“Have you asked?”

“Many people.”

“What did they say?”

“Different things.”

“Have you asked it?”

Lin looked at the cylinder.

Imogen opened her notebook again.

Mara felt foolish.

Then she remembered the fern requesting rain.

She leaned toward the glass.

“What would you like?”

For a moment, nothing changed.

Then the light became very small.

A dot.

A pinprick.

It moved to one side of the cylinder and waited.

Mara touched the glass.

The light followed her finger.

Imogen whispered, “Oh.”

Lin covered her mouth.

The dot moved again.

Toward the window.

Toward the garden.

---

The lid unscrewed.

That was all.

After twenty-seven years, the mechanism was no more complicated than a jar.

Lin turned it slowly.

A faint warmth rose from the opening.

The light drifted out.

It hung above the table.

Mara expected it to enlarge, to become dazzling, to announce what all this had meant.

Instead, it moved toward the garden door.

Imogen opened it.

Cold air entered.

The light passed between the women and out into the rain.

It hovered above the basin.

Then descended.

For an instant, the water shone.

A tiny green leaf appeared on the surface.

Then another.

The light was gone.

In its place floated a small water plant with three round leaves.

Mara looked at Lin.

Lin looked at the basin.

“That’s it?” she said.

The words were barely audible.

Imogen did not answer.

Mara crouched beside the water.

At the center of the leaves was a closed bud.

It was not remarkable.

It looked like something that might be overlooked in a garden center.

Lin knelt beside her.

Rain touched her hair.

“Twenty-seven years,” she whispered.

Mara had no answer that would not insult her.

So she stayed.

After a long time, Lin began to laugh.

Quietly at first.

Then helplessly.

Imogen brought out a towel.

Lin took it and wiped her face.

“I thought it would be a bridge,” she said. “Or a city. Or some enormous thing.”

“It might still become enormous,” Imogen said. “Plants can be dreadful that way.”

Lin laughed again.

Then she cried.

The three leaves shifted on the water.

The bud remained closed.

---

They found Lin a room above a café two streets away.

Imogen knew the owner.

Mara carried the empty case.

It weighed almost nothing.

At the café door, Lin took it from her.

“I don’t know what to do tomorrow.”

“Have breakfast,” Mara said.

Lin smiled.

“That seems small.”

“Yes.”

They said good night.

On the walk back, Imogen put her hands deep in her pockets.

“Do you think we handled that properly?”

“No idea.”

“Good.”

“Why good?”

“Beatrice says certainty is when most of the paperwork starts.”

They returned to the shop.

The garden was dark.

Mara checked the basin once more.

The plant floated near the edge.

Nothing about it explained the journey it had taken.

She found that she liked it better for that.

---

In the morning, there was a frog in the basin.

Bee discovered it.

She stood at the garden door and made a sound so piercing that Imogen dropped a spoon.

“Frog!”

Everyone came to look.

The frog sat beside the three leaves, round and solemn.

Arthur, visiting with a toolbox, said the courtyard had no obvious route from a pond.

Sami said frogs were good at jumping.

Arthur conceded the point.

Lin arrived just before noon.

She had slept.

It was visible in her face.

Mara showed her the frog.

Lin watched it for a long time.

Then she sat on the bench and opened a book.

She stayed until lunch.

No one asked her whether she had found a new purpose.

The frog jumped into the water.

The day continued.

---

Winter approached by degrees.

First the courtyard chairs became cold to sit on.

Then the vine thinned.

Then people began entering the shop with their shoulders raised against the weather, carrying little clouds of outside air in their coats.

The annex delivered a pair of dance shoes to a man with a walking stick.

A recipe written on the back of an electricity bill.

A train ticket dated forty years earlier, which turned out not to be an invitation to travel but evidence that someone had once tried.

Not all deliveries ended happily.

A woman returned a sealed envelope unopened.

A man broke a small porcelain bird and apologized with such desperation that Imogen had to take him into the kitchen and make him sit down.

Some things stayed on the shelf.

Some people did not come back.

Mara found this difficult.

Nadia, when asked, said, “So do we.”

“Does it get easier?”

“Not exactly.”

“Then how do you keep doing it?”

Nadia looked toward the children’s room.

Ada was teaching Bee how to fold a paper boat.

“By not making the outcome mine.”

Mara thought about that for several days.

She was still thinking about it when Jonah returned with his grandmother’s radio.

He had repaired it.

Mostly.

It received one station clearly and several others as a haunted murmur.

He set it on the counter.

“For the garden in summer,” he said.

Imogen looked at the radio.

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

He hesitated.

Then added, “I kept the tin.”

“Good.”

The radio played a song none of them recognized.

They listened anyway.

---

At the start of December, Mara received an envelope in the ordinary post.

No blue door.

No strange handwriting.

Just a stamp, a printed address, and the familiar inconvenience of something wedged beneath a pizza menu.

Inside was an invitation from a small local gallery.

Imogen had submitted photographs of the mural.

Without asking.

Mara called her immediately.

“You did what?”

“I filled out a form.”

“You didn’t tell me.”

“You would have spent three weeks deciding which photographs were inadequate.”

“That isn’t the point.”

“It’s part of the point.”

Mara sat at the kitchen table.

The invitation lay beside her.

“They want drawings.”

“You have drawings.”

“They’re not—”

“Finish that sentence carefully.”

Mara looked at the red notebook.

At the maps.

At the harbor and the impossible library and the mountain she still had not drawn properly.

“I’m frightened,” she said.

Imogen’s voice changed.

“I know.”

“What if they’re not good?”

“Then some of them won’t be good.”

“That’s your reassurance?”

“It’s the durable kind.”

Mara closed her eyes.

Then she opened the notebook.

“I’ll call them.”

“Good.”

“And don’t do that again.”

A pause.

“All right,” Imogen said.

Mara believed her.

That mattered too.

---

The exhibition occupied one wall.

Six drawings.

Not a career.

Not a revelation.

A wall.

Mara’s mother arrived first.

Arthur and Elspeth came next.

Ruth brought Peter, who had brought cake despite the gallery specifically requesting no food.

Ellis wore a jacket that looked borrowed.

Lin stood near the drawing of the courtyard basin.

Nadia came with Ada, who studied the map of the mountain and said the path was too obvious.

Imogen waited until everyone else was looking before touching Mara’s arm.

“You did it.”

Mara looked at the six frames.

“Yes.”

“How do you feel?”

“Like they’re all crooked.”

“They’re not.”

“The third one is.”

Imogen looked.

The third one was.

They straightened it together.

Later, a stranger bought the drawing of the listening house.

Mara almost told him he couldn’t.

Not because of the price.

Because she had not understood until that moment that making something could also mean letting it leave.

The man asked whether she minded telling him about the building.

She looked at the small crowd beside the refreshments.

At Ellis laughing with her mother.

At Ruth pretending not to see Peter cut the cake.

“It’s real,” she said.

The man waited.

“And it’s still being finished.”

He nodded.

“I like that.”

“So do I,” Mara said.

---

When the gallery closed, they walked to the bookshop.

The night was cold enough to make the city sharp.

Above the rooftops, clouds moved around a pale moon.

The shop windows glowed.

Imogen had left the railway lights on.

Inside, the painted river ran through its forest.

The fox watched from the wall.

In the garden, the basin held a thin skin of ice around the edges, but the three leaves remained green.

The blue flower in the stone had opened again.

This time, there was no bell.

Only a small seed.

Mara lifted it into her palm.

Ada leaned close.

“What is it?”

“I don’t know.”

“Are you going to plant it?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

Mara looked around the crowded shop.

At the books.

At the doors.

At all the people who had arrived by routes no map could have shown her a year before.

Then she closed her hand gently around the seed.

“Somewhere with room,” she said.

---

The next morning, the wall in Mara’s corridor needed touching up.

A narrow crack had appeared near the skirting board.

She mixed a little paint.

The color was still mustard left in the sun.

She no longer minded it.

As she worked, footsteps sounded on the stairs.

A new tenant stopped beside her carrying a box of kitchen things.

“Do you live here?”

“Upstairs.”

“I’m in two.”

“Welcome.”

The woman looked at the wall.

“Nice color.”

Mara considered correcting her.

Then smiled.

“It grows on you.”

The woman shifted the box.

“I’m Tessa.”

“Mara.”

“Do you know if the heating is always like this?”

“The radiator complains before it works.”

“Good to know.”

A small brass key fell from the box and struck the floor.

Tessa stared at it.

Mara put down her brush.

“Yours?”

“I don’t think so.”

Attached to the key was a paper label.

Tessa crouched and picked it up.

Her face changed as she read.

Mara did not ask what it said.

She waited.

After a moment, Tessa looked at the blank yellow wall.

“Has there,” she began, then stopped.

Mara leaned the brush across the paint tray.

“Sometimes,” she said.

Tessa looked at her.

A radiator knocked.

Somewhere in the building, a pipe sighed.

And very quietly, from behind a wall that had been perfectly ordinary a moment before, someone cleared their throat.

---

Tessa did not open the door that morning.

When it appeared, she looked at it once, looked at Mara, and said, “I have frozen food in here.”

Mara helped her carry the box upstairs.

They put peas, bread, and an alarming quantity of dumplings into the freezer.

Tessa set the key on the kitchen counter.

Then she made coffee.

The apartment was full of unassembled furniture and bags labeled with increasingly vague descriptions: **BATHROOM**, **BOOKS**, **OTHER BOOKS**, **THINGS FROM NEAR THE DOOR**.

Mara took the only chair.

Tessa sat on a box.

“Does this happen often?”

“Not usually twice before breakfast.”

Tessa looked at the key.

“My sister had one like this.”

Mara waited.

“She’s not dead,” Tessa added quickly.

“All right.”

“She lives in Glasgow.”

“People seem to end up quite far away.”

“We haven’t spoken for nine years.”

The kettle clicked off.

Tessa did not move.

“What did the label say?” Mara asked.

Tessa turned it over.

On the other side, in a cramped hand, was written:

**THE GREENHOUSE, IF YOU STILL REMEMBER.**

Mara looked toward the window.

The morning light touched the unopened boxes.

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

Tessa put the key facedown.

“Yes, I do.”

---

The greenhouse belonged to their grandfather.

It had stood at the bottom of a narrow garden behind a house in a town Mara had passed through often without ever getting off the train.

“He grew tomatoes,” Tessa said. “Badly. He talked about them as if they were difficult relatives.”

“Were they?”

“They refused to ripen.”

Tessa smiled.

Then the smile went.

“My sister and I used to hide there.”

“From what?”

“Mostly chores. Sometimes our parents.”

Mara drank her coffee.

“We made a time capsule,” Tessa continued. “A biscuit tin. We buried it beneath the potting bench.”

“When?”

“I was twelve. She was nine.”

“What was in it?”

“I remember a photograph. Some coins. A note.”

“Did you go back for it?”

“No.”

The key remained facedown between them.

“Granddad died. The house was sold. Then everything else happened.”

Mara could hear, in the phrase *everything else*, a country she had not been invited to enter.

She did not ask for its borders.

Tessa stood and poured the coffee.

“I don’t know who lives there now.”

“That can probably be found out.”

“I don’t know whether the greenhouse is still there.”

“That too.”

“I don’t know whether she’ll answer.”

Mara took the mug.

“No,” she said. “That part you won’t know first.”

---

Downstairs, the blue door was still waiting.

Tessa stood before it with her coat on.

Mara had washed her brush.

“Will you come in with me?”

“If you want.”

“Yes.”

They entered together.

The department smelled of paper and wet leaves.

Nadia sat behind the counter, sorting a tray of postcards.

She looked up.

“Mara.”

“Nadia.”

Tessa looked between them.

“Of course.”

Nadia smiled.

“We know each other from a bookshop.”

“That’s what all the ominous people say.”

“Usually they say a conference.”

Tessa laughed before she could prevent it.

Nadia put down the postcards.

“Tea?”

“No,” Tessa said. “Thank you. I’ve had coffee. I’d prefer to know what this is.”

She set the key on the counter.

Nadia examined the label.

Then she opened the ledger.

Unlike Ellis, she did not run a finger down the page.

She turned directly to the right place.

Mara wondered whether that was experience or a different sort of magic.

“Two recipients,” Nadia said.

Tessa’s hands tightened.

“Two?”

“Yes.”

“My sister?”

Nadia turned the ledger slightly.

There were two names.

**Tessa Ward.**  
**Leah Ward.**

Beside each was a small empty square.

“What do the boxes mean?”

“Whether the invitation has been received.”

“Has she?”

“Not yet.”

Tessa looked at the key.

“What if she doesn’t want to?”

“Then she doesn’t have to.”

“What if I don’t?”

“The same.”

Tessa was quiet.

Then she pointed to the empty square beside her own name.

“Do you tick that now?”

“Have you received the invitation?”

“I have the key.”

“That isn’t quite what I asked.”

Tessa looked toward the shelves.

A parcel rustled.

The fern shook a drop of water from one leaf.

At last she nodded.

“Yes.”

Nadia made a small mark.

Somewhere far away, so faintly that Mara might have imagined it, a telephone began to ring.

---

Leah did not receive a key.

She received a bus ticket.

This was inconvenient, because she had not taken a bus in that town for twenty-two years and no longer recognized the route number.

It appeared in the pocket of a coat she had been about to give away.

On the back was written:

**THE GREENHOUSE, IF YOU STILL REMEMBER.**

She read the words in her office during her lunch break.

Then she put the ticket in her purse and finished an email about a delayed shipment of laboratory glassware.

At three, she took it out again.

At four, she called her mother.

Her mother did not know who owned the old house.

At five, she found the address online.

At six, she stood in her kitchen holding her phone and looking at Tessa’s number.

It was old.

It might not work.

She had kept it anyway.

She put the phone down.

Picked it up.

Typed:

**Do you remember the greenhouse?**

Then she stared at the message until her screen dimmed.

In another city, Tessa sat on the floor among unopened boxes.

Her phone lit up.

For several seconds, she could not make herself touch it.

Mara, in the apartment below, heard footsteps begin and stop overhead.

She did not know what they meant.

She made dinner.

The world, she had learned, did not require her to witness every important thing.

---

Tessa replied:

**Yes.**

Then, after a minute:

**Did you get something strange?**

Leah’s answer arrived almost immediately.

**A bus ticket.**

Tessa laughed once.

Then she began to cry.

She typed:

**I got the key.**

For a long time, there was no reply.

Then:

**Of course you did. You were always in charge of the key.**

Tessa read the words three times.

They could have been an accusation.

Once, she would have made them one.

Instead, she wrote:

**I lost it twice.**

Leah replied:

**Three times. I didn’t tell you about the third.**

Tessa wiped her face.

In Glasgow, Leah sat down at her kitchen table.

Neither knew what to say next.

For once, neither mistook that for proof that nothing could be said.

---

They met on a Saturday.

Not at the greenhouse.

At a café near the station, because Leah said she was not beginning with damp glass and family archaeology before breakfast.

Tessa arrived early.

Leah arrived exactly on time.

They looked at one another across a small round table.

“You cut your hair,” Tessa said.

“Nine years ago.”

“Right.”

“You look tired.”

“I moved.”

“I know.”

Neither asked how the other knew.

They ordered coffee.

Then toast.

Then, because the coffee arrived too quickly and the silence needed something more substantial, eggs.

Outside, buses stopped and pulled away.

Inside, they began with practicalities.

Leah worked in procurement.

Tessa had left her job.

Their mother’s hip was worse than she admitted.

The old house belonged to a man named Anwar who had answered Leah’s email with unexpected kindness.

The greenhouse was still there.

By the time the food arrived, they had found three subjects on which they agreed and two on which they carefully did not.

It was not reconciliation.

It was breakfast.

For that morning, breakfast was enough.

---

Anwar met them at the gate wearing gardening gloves.

“I thought you might come together.”

“Did you?” Tessa asked.

“You both mentioned a sister and neither mentioned whether she was coming.”

He opened the gate.

The garden was smaller than they remembered.

This offended them both.

“The plum tree’s gone,” Leah said.

“Storm damage,” Anwar replied. “Before my time.”

“The path used to turn there.”

“It still does. The rosemary’s grown over it.”

They reached the greenhouse.

Its frame had been painted dark green.

One pane had been replaced with clear plastic.

Inside, tomato plants stood in large pots.

Tessa stopped.

“Do they ripen?”

Anwar looked puzzled.

“Usually.”

Leah laughed.

The sound startled both sisters.

Anwar pointed toward the potting bench.

“I haven’t moved it. I thought you should do that part.”

Then he left them alone.

The key fitted.

Tessa turned it.

The door opened with a familiar scrape.

For a moment, the years folded in a way that was almost cruel.

There was the warmth.

The smell of damp soil.

The narrow space where they had hidden from the world and from each other.

Leah stepped inside first.

“Three times,” she said.

“What?”

“You lost the key three times.”

Tessa smiled.

“Show-off.”

---

The biscuit tin was beneath a loose paving slab.

It had rusted at the edges.

They carried it outside and set it on the bench.

Tessa opened the lid.

Inside were two coins, a dried flower, a plastic ring, a photograph, and an envelope wrapped in what had once been a bread bag.

Leah picked up the ring.

“I thought you stole this.”

“I thought you gave it to me.”

“I didn’t.”

“Oh.”

They looked at one another.

Then both began to laugh.

Not because it was particularly funny.

Because there was something almost unbearable about discovering that even their ancient grievances contained clerical errors.

The photograph showed them beside their grandfather.

Tessa wore a serious expression.

Leah had one eye shut.

Their grandfather held a tomato that appeared, after all, to have ripened.

On the back, he had written:

**Proof.**

Tessa handed it to Leah.

Then she opened the envelope.

Inside were two notes.

Her own was written in careful sentences.

Leah’s sprawled across the page in enormous letters.

Tessa read hers aloud.

> Dear future us,
>
> I hope we live in a house with a dog. I hope we have enough money for heating all the time. I hope Mum and Dad stop shouting. I will look after Leah, even when she is annoying.
>
> We should remember Granddad says tomatoes take as long as they take.

Leah stared at the paper.

Tessa lowered it.

“I’d forgotten.”

“I hadn’t,” Leah said.

Tessa turned toward her.

Leah was looking at the sentence about being looked after.

For a moment, the old argument stood between them, fully formed, ready to resume.

Then Leah picked up her own note.

She read:

> Dear when we are old,
>
> I hope Tessa gets a horse.
>
> She says she doesn’t want one now but she does.
>
> I hope she stops being Mum when Mum is sad.
>
> I hope I am allowed to help.
>
> Do not forget me if you go somewhere good.

Tessa pressed her hand to her mouth.

The greenhouse door moved softly in the wind.

Beyond the wall, a bus passed.

Leah folded the note.

“I was angry,” she said, “because you kept deciding what I could bear.”

Tessa looked down at the photograph.

“I thought that was how to keep you safe.”

“I know.”

“I didn’t know how to stop.”

“I know that too.”

Neither forgave the other in a single sentence.

There were things to explain.

Things to regret.

Things neither could yet say without becoming unfair.

But when Anwar returned with tea, they were sitting on the same bench.

The tin rested open between them.

---

Tessa told Mara only part of the story.

They met in the corridor on Sunday evening.

Mara was carrying laundry.

Tessa held a small tomato plant in a paper bag.

“Anwar gave us one each.”

“Who’s Anwar?”

“The man with the greenhouse.”

“Right.”

Tessa looked down at the leaves.

“I don’t know where to put it.”

“Sunny window.”

“My kitchen gets morning light.”

“That should help.”

They stood beside the yellow wall.

The blue door was gone.

There was no hook.

No key.

No message.

Tessa touched the paper bag.

“It wasn’t simple.”

“No.”

“We’re meeting again next month.”

“Good.”

“I think she still hates me a little.”

Mara shifted the laundry basket.

“Maybe ask her.”

Tessa laughed.

“That sounds dangerous.”

“Yes.”

Upstairs, someone dropped something heavy.

A voice swore.

The radiator knocked.

Ordinary life carried on with its usual lack of delicacy.

Tessa stepped toward the stairs.

“Mara?”

“Yes?”

“Thank you for not telling me to open it.”

Mara thought about that after Tessa had gone.

Then she carried the laundry down to the machines and discovered she had forgotten the detergent.

Some things, apparently, remained unfinished because she was carrying too much at once.

---

At the bookshop, the railway’s blue door opened that night without a train passing.

A folded map slid out onto the platform.

Imogen found it at closing.

She almost put it beneath a mug.

Then she saw her own name.

Not on a label.

Written across the map itself.

A road ran through the letters.

She unfolded it carefully.

The paper was old, thick, and soft at the creases.

It showed a town she did not recognize.

There was a square.

A river.

A row of narrow houses.

A bookshop.

Above the bookshop, a room had been marked with a red circle.

At the bottom of the page was a note.

**You may know where you are and still not know what you are staying for.**

Imogen read it.

Then she turned the map over.

On the back was a much smaller message.

**No, this does not invalidate the opening-hours agreement.**

She sat down behind the counter.

For several minutes, she did not move.

Then she called Mara.

“Are you busy?”

“Eating.”

“Good. Finish eating.”

“Why?”

“I think I’ve been delivered to.”

---

Mara arrived forty minutes later with a container of soup.

Imogen let her in.

The shop was quiet.

The railway lights were off.

The map lay open on the counter.

Mara set down the soup and studied it.

“Do you know the town?”

“No.”

“The room?”

“No.”

“The bookshop?”

Imogen hesitated.

“It looks like the first one I ever worked in.”

“Where?”

“North coast. Small town. I was nineteen.”

“Was it this town?”

“No. I’d know.”

Mara looked more closely.

The street names were blank.

The buildings had been drawn with extraordinary detail, but the whole town seemed to resist recognition.

“What were you doing there?”

“Working.”

“Besides that.”

Imogen leaned against the shelf.

“Waiting.”

“For what?”

“For my sister to finish an apprenticeship. We were going to travel.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“The shop owner got ill. I stayed to help.”

“And then?”

“He got better. I stayed because I liked the work.”

“That sounds reasonable.”

“It was.”

“Your sister?”

“She went.”

“The one in Bellwether?”

“Yes.”

Mara looked at the map.

“Do you regret staying?”

Imogen opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Then said, “I don’t know whether I’ve ever asked myself without immediately preparing a defense.”

Mara sat on the stool beside her.

They ate the soup while it was still warm.

---

The map changed overnight.

When Imogen unfolded it the next morning, the bookshop had moved.

It now stood beside the river.

The red-circled room remained above it.

She called Mara.

Mara was halfway up a ladder.

“Can it wait?”

“Yes,” Imogen said after a moment. “It can.”

“Lunch?”

“Lunch.”

By noon, a bridge had appeared.

By one, the square was gone.

Mara studied the paper while eating a sandwich.

“Arthur.”

Imogen nodded.

“Arthur.”

He answered on the second ring.

They described the map.

There was a long pause.

Then he said, “Don’t draw on it.”

Mara lowered her pencil.

“Why?”

“Because that may be a prospective map.”

“A what?”

“A map of places that have not decided whether to exist.”

Imogen looked at the river.

The ink moved, almost imperceptibly.

“Why does it have my name on it?”

Arthur sighed.

“Ah.”

“Arthur.”

“Yes?”

“What does ‘ah’ mean?”

“It means I need my blue coat.”

---

He arrived the next day with Elspeth and a magnifying glass.

Elspeth went directly to the garden.

Arthur bent over the map.

“Hm.”

Imogen folded her arms.

“That’s not better than ‘ah.’”

“No.”

He examined the red circle.

Then the blank street names.

Then the tiny bookshop windows.

At last he straightened.

“It’s a proposal.”

“For what?”

“A place.”

“I gathered that.”

“Not a place to go. A place to make.”

Mara looked at Imogen.

Imogen looked at the map.

“I already have one.”

“Yes,” Arthur said.

“Then why—”

“Because perhaps you’ve been treating it as something you inherited from your earlier decisions, rather than something you are still choosing.”

Imogen was quiet.

Arthur removed his spectacles.

“That was speculation. I dislike sounding certain about somebody else’s map.”

Mara appreciated him for saying it.

“What’s the room?” she asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Could we go there?”

“Possibly.”

“How?”

Arthur looked at the model railway.

Then very deliberately looked away.

“No.”

Imogen smiled.

“You thought of something.”

“Yes. I also thought better of it.”

From the garden, Elspeth called, “A promising novelty.”

---

They did not use the railway.

They used the front door.

Arthur said the distinction mattered.

At closing time, Imogen laid the map on the counter and placed one hand over the red circle.

“What am I supposed to do?”

“Choose a direction.”

“On the map?”

“No.”

Imogen looked toward the garden.

Then the children’s room.

Then the street beyond the glass.

“I want to know what I’m staying for.”

Arthur nodded.

“That will do.”

She opened the front door.

Outside was not their street.

It was the square from the first version of the map.

Rain shone on pale paving stones.

Across the square stood the bookshop.

Its windows were dark.

Above them, one room glowed.

Imogen held the door.

Mara looked at Arthur.

“Coming?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

He touched the folded timetable in his coat pocket.

“I have somewhere else to be.”

Elspeth took his arm.

They stepped past Imogen into the ordinary street, which appeared for them as naturally as it had disappeared for her.

Mara watched them walk toward the station.

Then she looked through the doorway again.

The unfamiliar square remained.

Imogen took a breath.

“Five minutes?”

Mara smiled.

“That’s what you said the first time.”

They crossed the threshold together.

---

The town was empty, but not abandoned.

This distinction became clear as they walked.

The gutters were clean.

Flowers grew in window boxes.

Bicycles stood beside railings.

A café had cups laid out on its tables, though no one sat there and no steam rose from the coffee machine.

“It’s waiting,” Mara said.

Imogen glanced at her.

“Everyone keeps saying that.”

“Maybe lots of things are.”

They reached the other bookshop.

Its sign was blank.

The door opened at Imogen’s touch.

Inside were shelves.

Empty shelves.

A counter.

A ladder.

A window overlooking the river.

The room was beautiful.

Mara saw Imogen notice it.

Then resist noticing it.

“This is unfair.”

“What?”

“It’s exactly the right size.”

“For what?”

“Everything.”

They walked between the shelves.

There was space for readings.

Space for children.

Space for people to sit without feeling they ought to buy something.

A door at the back led to a courtyard larger than the one they had made.

No drainage problem.

No cracked basin.

No awkward wall.

Imogen stood in the center.

“I hate it.”

Mara waited.

“No,” Imogen said. “That’s not true.”

“What is true?”

“I want it.”

The words seemed to frighten her.

Mara looked around the empty room.

“That’s allowed.”

“I love the shop.”

“I know.”

“I’ve worked so hard.”

“I know.”

“Wanting this feels like saying that wasn’t enough.”

Mara thought of her father’s tape.

The six drawings.

The empty pages in the red notebook.

“Maybe it means you’re still here,” she said. “Wanting things.”

Imogen sat on the edge of the counter.

For a long time, she looked at the shelves.

Then, above them, a floorboard creaked.

---

The stairs were behind a narrow door.

At the top was the room marked on the map.

It contained a table, two chairs, and a window.

On the table lay a ledger.

Imogen stopped.

“Oh, absolutely not.”

Mara laughed.

The ledger opened by itself.

Imogen folded her arms.

“No.”

A page turned.

Then another.

Words appeared across the paper.

**THIS IS NOT AN APPLICATION FOR EMPLOYMENT.**

“That’s a start,” Imogen said.

Below it, another line formed.

**WHAT WOULD YOU KEEP?**

The room went still.

Mara looked at the window.

Outside, the town had changed again.

The square was now a narrow lane.

The river ran closer.

A bridge stood where there had been houses.

Imogen approached the ledger.

“What would I keep from the shop?”

No answer appeared.

She picked up the pencil beside the book.

Then put it down.

“I don’t like quizzes with invisible marking schemes.”

Mara sat in one of the chairs.

“Then don’t treat it like a quiz.”

Imogen looked at her.

“What would you keep?”

Mara thought.

“The window in the storeroom.”

A line appeared in the ledger.

**THE WINDOW.**

Imogen stared.

“The children’s room,” she said.

**THE CHILDREN’S ROOM.**

“The garden.”

**THE GARDEN.**

“The way people come in to get out of the rain and end up staying.”

The pencil moved by itself.

**SHELTER WITHOUT AN IMMEDIATE PRICE.**

Imogen swallowed.

“The books.”

The page remained blank beneath the last line.

She frowned.

“The books,” she repeated.

Nothing.

Mara looked at the empty shelves below.

“What about them?”

Imogen rested both hands on the table.

“The right one finding the right person.”

The words appeared.

**DISCOVERY.**

They continued.

The table filled with things that were not furniture.

Trust.

Argument without humiliation.

Children allowed to be loud sometimes.

Adults allowed to be uncertain.

Somewhere to sit.

The chance to begin again without making a speech about it.

At last, Imogen stopped.

The ledger turned a page.

A new question appeared.

**WHAT WOULD YOU CHANGE?**

Imogen began to laugh.

Then she picked up the pencil.

---

The list was longer than she expected.

Not because she disliked her life.

Because she had rarely allowed herself to imagine improving it without first proving that improvement was necessary.

She wanted one day off that remained a day off.

She wanted to visit her sister without calculating how many sales she would lose.

She wanted a proper salary.

She wanted the shop to survive if she became ill.

She wanted help she did not have to apologize for needing.

Mara watched the words fill the page.

Outside, windows lit across the town.

At the bottom, Imogen wrote:

**I want this to be a choice, not a test of how much I can endure.**

The pencil stopped.

For a moment, the room seemed to breathe.

Then the ledger closed.

On its cover appeared a title.

**A WORKING PLAN.**

Imogen stared.

“That’s it?”

Mara smiled.

“Were you hoping for a prophecy?”

“Possibly a grant.”

The window opened.

A breeze entered, carrying the smell of rain and paper.

Below them, the empty shelves began to fill.

Not with books.

With outlines.

Possible books.

Possible chairs.

Possible people.

None remained long.

The town was not offering her a replacement life.

It was making room for her to see the one she had.

Imogen picked up the ledger.

It was heavy.

Ordinarily heavy.

The kind of weight that required both hands.

“I think,” she said, “we should go home.”

---

They returned through the front door at 6:17.

Only seventeen minutes had passed.

Mara checked her phone.

“That’s convenient.”

Imogen set the ledger on the counter.

“Don’t praise it. It’ll get ideas.”

The map lay where they had left it.

The town had vanished.

In its place was a drawing of their own street.

The bookshop.

The courtyard.

The narrow room above the neighboring café, marked with a red circle.

Imogen frowned.

“Lin’s room?”

Mara looked closer.

“No. Next door.”

A small line of writing appeared at the bottom.

**Sometimes more room is available by ordinary inquiry.**

Imogen stared at it.

Then she began to laugh so hard she had to sit down.

---

The room next door was vacant.

So was the one beneath it.

The café owner had been using them for storage, badly.

Imogen knew the feeling.

They spoke on Monday.

By Friday, there was a proposal.

Not a miracle.

A lease.

It involved figures that made Mara’s eyes glaze over and Imogen become unusually alert.

Arthur reviewed the layout.

Elspeth reviewed the exit clauses.

Nadia said the annex could contribute a modest fee for the use of the threshold.

Beatrice sent a form.

Imogen read it.

Then crossed out three lines.

For the first time, Beatrice wrote back with no objections.

The bookshop did not become enormous.

It gained a room.

A proper event space.

A table where people could work.

A small office with a door Imogen could close.

And enough income, eventually—Arthur had by then repaired the railway—for a part-time bookseller named Farah, who knew more about poetry than anyone and refused to regard the blue door as more troublesome than the stock database.

On her first day, a parcel materialized beside the till.

Farah looked at it.

“Is this incoming or customer collection?”

Imogen smiled.

“I think you’ll be fine.”

---

Mara’s own map remained unfinished.

She added to it slowly.

The city of bridges became less impossible when she learned about real cities built around water.

The library that regrew its books became a series of drawings about memory.

The mountain of truth became a hill with several paths and a note in the margin:

**Do not confuse difficulty with importance.**

She crossed the note out.

It sounded too much like a department.

Then she wrote it again on a separate page because, irritatingly, it remained useful.

At night, she sometimes played the tape.

Not every night.

Some weeks she forgot.

The first time she realized she had not listened for a month, guilt rose so quickly that she nearly put it on at once.

Instead, she sat still.

She thought of her father at the kitchen table, pressing the red button, making mistakes, leaving room for laughter.

He had wanted an expedition.

Not a vigil.

She put the player back on the shelf.

Then she called her mother to ask whether she wanted to visit the gallery on Saturday.

---

Spring returned to the courtyard.

The frog returned too, or a frog sufficiently similar that no one felt qualified to challenge it.

The plant in the basin produced a white flower.

Lin came to see it.

She had found a flat nearby and a job three days a week cataloging old photographs.

The empty leather case sat beneath her desk.

“What do you keep in it now?” Mara asked.

“Sandwiches, mostly.”

“Good use.”

“I think so.”

They sat beside the basin.

The flower opened further in the sun.

“Do you miss traveling?”

“Sometimes.”

“Will you go again?”

“Yes.”

“With the case?”

“Probably.”

Mara looked at the three leaves.

“Do you ever wish it had become something else?”

Lin considered.

“A little.”

She smiled.

“I’m trying not to punish it for that.”

The frog moved.

The flower trembled.

Inside the shop, someone rang the ordinary bell at the counter.

Lin stood.

“I’ll get it.”

---

On the anniversary of the blue door’s first appearance, Mara painted over the last unpainted rectangle in the corridor.

She did not mark the date deliberately.

She noticed it on an invoice after she had already cleaned her brush.

A year.

The wall looked much the same.

The building sounded much the same.

Mr. Vale still watered his plants at seven when he was home, though he was away more often now.

Tessa’s tomato plant had died.

She and Leah had found this funny in a way Mara suspected would have baffled their grandfather.

Mara’s rooms still contained unpaid bills sometimes.

Her windowsill plant remained unimpressed by the available light.

The red notebook was half full.

She stood in the corridor and looked at the wall.

For a moment, she wondered whether she ought to say thank you.

To whom, she was not sure.

Then she heard someone struggling with the front door downstairs.

She went to help.

A courier stood on the step with a large box.

“Finch?”

“Yes.”

“Signature.”

She signed.

The box contained a framed photograph.

The listening house at dusk.

Windows open.

A crowd on the grass.

The kite overhead.

On the back, Ellis had written:

**The high reed still sticks.**

Below it, Ruth had added:

**He fixes it. Then we fly it anyway.**

Mara carried the photograph upstairs.

She hung it where the evening light would reach it.

Then she went out to meet her friends.

---

At the bookshop, Farah was reading a poem to a child who insisted it needed a dragon.

“It’s about a pear,” Farah said.

“The dragon could eat the pear.”

“That would be a different poem.”

“Then read that one.”

Imogen was in the office with the door closed.

This pleased Mara more than seeing her at the counter would have.

In the garden, Arthur and Elspeth were planning a journey.

Not for the department.

For themselves.

A paper timetable lay between them.

Peter had sent lemon cake.

Ruth’s note said this represented a significant philosophical concession.

Nadia sat beneath the vine, drinking tea.

Ada had grown taller.

She was helping Bee build a cardboard city with tunnels beneath it.

Mara sat beside Nadia.

“How’s the department?”

“Busy.”

“Always?”

“Usually.”

“Do you ever finish?”

Nadia looked around the courtyard.

At the books passing from hand to hand.

At the repaired gate.

At the little flower in the basin.

“Not in the way people mean.”

Mara nodded.

Above them, the wind instrument made a soft sound.

Not a conclusion.

Just a change in the air.

---

Later, when most of the guests had left, Mara found Ada beside the railway.

The little station lights were on.

A paper boat sat on the platform.

Ada looked up.

“Do you think there’s an end?”

“To the track?”

“No.”

Mara considered.

The locomotive moved through the cutting.

“There are ends to things.”

Ada nodded.

“Books.”

“Yes.”

“Days.”

“Yes.”

“People.”

Mara rested her hand on the edge of the table.

“Yes.”

Ada watched the train cross the bridge.

“Then why does the department keep going?”

Mara thought of the shelves.

The crates.

The things people had not said.

The things they had said badly and hoped to try again.

“Because an ending doesn’t use everything up.”

Ada looked at her.

“That sounds like something my mum would say.”

“Is that bad?”

“Not always.”

The train returned to the station.

Its light shone through the little blue door.

Ada picked up the paper boat and placed it carefully beside the painted river.

“Can we make another map?”

Mara smiled.

“Yes.”

“What of?”

They looked toward the garden.

Beyond it, the evening city spread in streets and windows and rooms full of people whose stories had not yet reached the shop.

Mara brought out the red notebook.

She opened it to a blank page.

Ada took the pencil.

“First,” she said, “we need to decide where the river goes.”

Mara leaned closer.

And together they began to draw.

---

They drew until the daylight thinned.

Ada wanted the river to divide around an island.

Mara asked what was on the island.

“A house.”

“What sort of house?”

“One people can leave.”

“That’s most houses.”

“Not if they think they have to stay.”

Mara looked at her.

Ada continued drawing.

She added windows.

A garden.

A path to the water.

Then, very carefully, two doors.

“One to come in,” she said. “One to go out.”

“Can’t the same door do both?”

“Yes. But sometimes people need to see the difference.”

From the counter, Nadia called her name.

Ada set down the pencil.

“Don’t finish it without me.”

“I won’t.”

“Promise?”

Mara looked at the half-drawn island.

“Yes.”

Ada ran to collect her coat.

Mara remained at the table.

The house had no roof yet.

The river vanished beneath Ada’s sleeve mark.

There were eraser crumbs on the page.

She brushed them away and closed the notebook.

Some unfinished things were not burdens.

Some were invitations to return.

---

That night, a letter arrived for Imogen through the model station.

It was ordinary in every respect except its route.

White envelope.

Black ink.

No shifting map.

No mysterious instruction.

Inside was a request from a woman named Saira.

She wanted to borrow the event room for an evening.

The subject was listed as:

**How to Start Again When You Would Rather Not Call It That.**

Imogen read the letter twice.

Then she telephoned the number at the bottom.

Saira answered while washing dishes.

“Yes?”

“This is Imogen. From the bookshop.”

“Oh. I wasn’t sure that would work.”

“What?”

“The address.”

“Where did you send it?”

“To the place that might say yes.”

Imogen looked at the envelope.

Beneath the shop’s name, where the street should have been, someone had written:

**Near enough, if there is room.**

Imogen sat down.

“What sort of evening did you have in mind?”

“Nothing grand.”

“Good.”

“Some chairs. Tea. People who’ve had to change something.”

“That’s most people.”

“Yes,” Saira said. “That was what I thought.”

---

The first gathering had seven people.

The second had twelve.

The third had nine, because rain had flooded the bus route and one person had changed their mind at the door.

Saira did not give advice unless asked.

She did not call it healing.

She brought a kettle and a packet of inexpensive biscuits and asked each person what had brought them there.

Some answered.

Some passed.

A retired nurse said she did not know who she was when no one needed her on a ward.

A young father said he missed sleeping and then apologized for saying something so small.

An older man said he had learned to cook one meal and was tired of it.

A woman in a green coat said nothing for three weeks, then told them she had bought a train ticket.

Mara attended once.

She sat near the back.

When her turn came, she said, “I used to think finishing meant not feeling it anymore.”

No one interrupted.

She looked at the cup in her hands.

“I don’t think that now.”

Saira nodded.

“What do you think?”

Mara considered the question.

Outside the room, the bookshop was quiet.

Beyond that, the railway stood with its tiny lights.

“I think it means making enough room for something else.”

The retired nurse smiled.

The young father yawned.

Someone laughed gently.

The evening continued.

---

Afterward, Mara helped stack the chairs.

Saira wiped the table.

“How did you find the shop?” Mara asked.

“A woman gave me a bell.”

Mara stopped.

“A small brass one?”

“Yes.”

“What was written on it?”

Saira smiled.

“Something about being ready to be answered.”

“What happened when you rang it?”

“I heard a train.”

Mara looked toward the storeroom.

“Of course you did.”

“Does that mean something?”

“Yes,” Mara said. “But not necessarily anything you need to worry about.”

Saira folded the cloth.

“I was told this place had room.”

Mara lifted the last chair.

“It’s learning.”

---

In the department, far beyond any street Mara knew, Beatrice stood before a wall of maps.

Some were old enough to have cracked at the folds.

Some were still forming.

Across one district, a new line had appeared.

It ran from a yellow wall to a coastal headland.

From there to a bookshop.

Then outward.

To Norwich.

To Glasgow.

To a greenhouse.

To a garden basin.

To rooms that did not yet have names.

Nadia stood beside her.

“Busy junction.”

“Yes.”

“Too busy?”

Beatrice considered.

On one map, a tiny light moved along a railway.

On another, a paper boat entered a river.

“No,” she said.

Nadia smiled.

Beatrice adjusted her spectacles.

“But we should send them another kettle.”

---

The kettle arrived on Wednesday.

Imogen opened the parcel.

Farah read the label.

**CAPACITY SUPPORT. NO ADDITIONAL OBLIGATION IMPLIED.**

“That’s unusually considerate,” Farah said.

Imogen plugged it in.

The kettle held exactly as much water as they needed and no more.

Arthur examined it during his next visit.

“That’s impossible.”

Farah handed him a mug.

“So is the stock database after an update. We adapt.”

The kettle became part of the kitchen.

No one built a shrine to it.

It was descaled regularly.

Mara thought the department might approve.

---

The island map grew slowly.

Ada returned on Saturdays.

Sometimes they worked for an hour.

Sometimes they did not touch it.

They added a footbridge.

A ferry.

A grove of trees whose names they invented and later changed because Bee objected to the botany.

The house acquired a roof.

Then a kitchen.

Then a room with no stated purpose.

“What happens there?” Mara asked.

Ada shrugged.

“Depends who comes.”

They drew a shelf beside the door.

“For things people put down.”

“A coat rack?”

“Some coats. Other things.”

Mara drew a small brass hook.

Ada nodded.

“Like that.”

In the garden of the island house, they planted the seed from the blue flower.

Not the real seed.

A drawn one.

The real one remained in a little paper envelope in Mara’s notebook.

She had not found the right place.

This no longer worried her.

---

One Saturday, Ada did not come.

Nadia telephoned.

“Fever. Nothing serious. She’s furious.”

“Tell her the map can wait.”

“She says you’ll add sensible things while she’s gone.”

“I was thinking of a drainage system.”

There was a distant groan.

Mara laughed.

“Tell her I won’t.”

She closed the notebook and put it away.

Then she helped Farah unpack a delivery of books.

An ordinary delivery.

Wrong quantities.

Damaged corner.

Three copies of a title nobody remembered ordering.

They dealt with it.

Later, Jonah came in with a new battery for the garden radio.

Tessa arrived carrying tomatoes.

Small ones.

Red.

She set them on the counter with the gravity of a scientific breakthrough.

“Proof,” she said.

Mara smiled.

Outside, the afternoon widened.

There was more than one way to keep a promise.

---

When Ada returned, she brought a drawing made in bed.

A boat.

Small, square, with a roof and a stove pipe.

“It’s for people who aren’t ready to get off.”

Mara studied it.

“How does it reach the island?”

“The river.”

“What if they don’t want to go there?”

“Then it goes past.”

Mara looked at the child.

Ada was already opening the notebook.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You’re doing the look.”

“What look?”

“The grown-up look when you think someone’s said something.”

Mara laughed.

“All right.”

They added the boat.

Its name, painted on the side, was **Not Yet**.

---

The map did not become real all at once.

At first, Mara only dreamed of the river.

Not every night.

A broad gray ribbon beneath a pale sky.

A bank of reeds.

The sound of a boat moving slowly against the current.

Then Ada mentioned the same river.

They compared notes without intending to.

The bend.

The island.

The house.

The unfinished path.

Nadia listened with an expression Mara had learned to recognize.

It was the expression department employees wore when they were trying to decide whether something required a form.

“Should we stop drawing?” Mara asked.

“Do you want to?”

“No.”

“Then let me ask someone.”

“Beatrice?”

“Probably.”

“Will she lecture?”

“Almost certainly.”

Mara sighed.

Nadia smiled.

“She worries.”

---

Beatrice came on a Thursday.

She examined the map.

She examined the seed.

She examined Mara.

Then Ada.

At last she removed her spectacles.

“This is not entirely unprecedented.”

“Leeds?” Mara asked.

“No. Different principle.”

“What principle?”

“Invitation made habitable.”

Ada leaned forward.

“Is that bad?”

“No.”

“Then why do you look cross?”

“Because it is almost always attempted by people who haven’t considered sanitation.”

Mara glanced at Ada.

Ada looked away.

Beatrice put her spectacles back on.

“A place like this requires boundaries.”

“We have doors.”

“More than doors.”

“Opening hours?” Mara asked.

“Among other things.”

They sat at the table.

Together, they made a list.

No one could be brought there against their will.

No one had to stay.

No one had to explain themselves to receive shelter.

No one could demand that another person remain.

There had to be ordinary food.

Ordinary sleep.

A way to return.

A way to ask for help.

Ada wrote the last rule herself.

**The house must not get lonely and trick people into staying.**

Beatrice read it.

Then nodded.

“A very good rule.”

---

They still needed sanitation.

Arthur was delighted.

Elspeth made him promise not to miss their train.

He drew a system so elegant that Mara suspected he had been waiting all his life to solve plumbing for a place that had not yet decided to exist.

Farah suggested shelves.

Imogen suggested a fund.

Ruth suggested sturdy shoes by the door.

Peter suggested cake and was overruled only on the question of whether it counted as a balanced meal.

Lin suggested that the shelf for burdens should not be too large.

“Why?”

“People will assume they have to fill it.”

Jonah suggested a radio.

Tessa suggested a greenhouse.

Leah, visiting for the weekend, suggested putting the greenhouse somewhere the tomatoes would actually ripen.

The map filled.

Not crowded.

Considered.

There remained a blank space at the center of the garden.

Mara took out the real seed.

It lay in her palm, small and dark.

Ada looked at Beatrice.

“Now?”

Beatrice studied the page.

Then the people around it.

“Perhaps,” she said, “now.”

---

Mara set the seed on the drawn garden.

Nothing happened.

Peter cleared his throat.

“Water?”

Ruth gave him a look.

“It was a practical suggestion.”

Mara smiled.

Ada placed one finger beside the seed.

“Not yet?” she asked.

The seed shifted.

Only slightly.

Then a thin root emerged and entered the paper.

The map did not tear.

Green rose from the page.

A stem.

Two leaves.

The smell of rain filled the room.

For one moment, everyone saw the island.

Not as a drawing.

As a place.

The river moved around it.

The house stood beneath a brightening sky.

A window was open.

Somewhere inside, a kettle began to boil.

Then the vision folded.

On the table lay the map.

At its center stood a tiny living tree.

Beatrice closed her folder.

“Well,” she said.

No one asked her to finish the sentence.

---

The first person to reach the island was not any of them.

It was a woman on the night bus.

She had worked a double shift, missed her stop, and found herself too tired to be angry.

Rain moved across the windows.

The bus was nearly empty.

At the next stop, the driver looked at her in the mirror.

“Here?”

She did not recognize the road.

Beyond the shelter, a light shone beside water.

“Is this the terminus?”

“Not exactly.”

She should have been frightened.

Later, she would wonder why she wasn’t.

Perhaps because the driver sounded ordinary.

Perhaps because the light reminded her of a kitchen.

Perhaps because she had spent so long needing nothing that, for once, she could not keep up the effort.

She stepped off the bus.

A path led through reeds.

At its end waited a small boat.

On the side, in uneven letters, was painted:

**NOT YET.**

A person in a thick coat stood beside it.

“Island?”

“What island?”

“The one with the house.”

She looked across the water.

A window glowed.

“Do I have to?”

“No.”

“Can I sit here instead?”

The person moved a bag from the bench.

“Of course.”

She sat.

For several minutes, she listened to the river.

No one asked her name.

No one asked what had happened.

When she was ready, someone handed her a cup of tea.

---

In the bookshop, the little tree put out a new leaf.

Mara noticed while washing mugs.

She went to the table.

Ada had written a sign beside the map.

**PLEASE DO NOT MOVE THE ISLAND WHILE DUSTING.**

Below it, in Beatrice’s handwriting, someone had added:

**This is not a metaphor.**

Mara touched the edge of the page.

For a moment, she heard water.

A cup set down.

A tired person breathing more slowly.

Then the ordinary sounds of the shop returned.

Imogen looked up from the counter.

“Everything all right?”

Mara listened once more.

“Yes,” she said.

Then she dried the mug and put it on the shelf.

---

The house on the island required a keeper.

Everyone had expected this to become complicated.

It did.

Not because no one volunteered.

Because too many people did.

Lin offered one week a month.

Saira offered weekends.

Ellis said he could come during the winter, when work at the listening house slowed.

Nadia reminded them that no one should confuse helping with disappearing into usefulness.

Imogen wrote that down.

Mara said she would visit, but she did not want to live there.

For a moment, she felt she ought to apologize.

Then she remembered the rules.

So she did not.

They made a rota.

It was imperfect.

People swapped days.

Trains were delayed.

Someone forgot the bread.

The island remained.

Its magic did not eliminate the need for groceries.

Ruth, hearing this, said she could have told them that.

---

Mara’s first crossing happened on an ordinary afternoon.

She entered the storeroom to fetch a chair.

The blue door stood open.

Beyond it lay the river.

Not the department.

Not the square.

The river.

At the landing, the **Not Yet** rocked gently against its rope.

Lin sat beneath the boat’s little roof, eating a sandwich.

She looked up.

“Coming?”

“For how long?”

“How long have you got?”

Mara checked her phone.

“I need to be back by six.”

“Then we’ll be back by six.”

“That sounds suspiciously convenient.”

“Only if we leave now.”

Mara set down the chair.

Then she stepped through.

The air smelled of water and wet wood.

Behind her, the bookshop remained visible through the doorway.

Ahead, the island waited.

Not calling.

Not demanding.

Just there.

She climbed into the boat.

Lin untied the rope.

They moved out into the current.

---

The island was smaller than Mara expected.

Everything important in her life seemed to have developed that habit.

The house had a red roof.

Ada had chosen it.

The front door was blue.

Nobody admitted whose idea that had been.

Shoes stood in pairs beneath a bench.

Some new.

Some worn.

All clean.

Inside, there was a kitchen, a long table, a room with books, and a quiet room with a window facing the river.

Calder Row was not old enough to be picturesque.

That, Tomas said, had helped make it expendable.

The houses had been built for railway workers in the years before the First World War, cheaply and in a hurry. They had small rooms, steep stairs, narrow yards, and gutters that required more attention than anyone in charge of maintenance had recently been willing to give.

“It wasn’t a good place to live anymore.”

“Because of the houses?” Mara asked.

“Because of several things.”

He did not try to simplify it.

She respected that.

The redevelopment would contain more homes.

Some would be affordable.

Some would not.

There would be a public square, a nursery, trees selected by someone who understood roots, and underground drainage that Arthur would probably have admired.

The shared turn of heads.

The redevelopment was not transformed by Monday.

There was no sudden generosity at the sight of a moving photograph.

Tomas met with the project director.

Then the planning consultant.

Then someone responsible for the financial model, who said the word *precedent* as if it were a disease.

Then a cost estimate.

Then letters from former residents.

The path was approved.

A public bench was approved.

The sycamore could not be saved in place because its roots had damaged a retaining wall.

A young tree would be planted from its seed.

Some people disliked that.

They were entitled to.

The doorstep was incorporated into the entrance to the community room.

The goalposts were painted on a new wall.

She told Mara this was a relief.

The memory of Calder Row went into the public library.

Not the department.

The library had a local history archive, a scanner, staff who understood consent, and a budget that needed defending every year.

Farah helped organize the recordings.

Residents chose which stories could be shared.

Some photographs were labeled.

Some were withdrawn.

Some remained unidentified.

Mara drew a map of the back passage.

Not an accurate map.

An inhabited one.

It marked the places where food had been left, where children had crossed between yards, where arguments had started and occasionally ended.

At the bottom, she wrote:

**Based on the memories of people who disagree about several details.**

The archivist smiled.

“That’s more honest than most maps.”

Arthur, when shown it, said he had always maintained the same.

Elspeth raised an eyebrow.

He amended this to “often.”

---

Tomas came to the bookshop on a wet afternoon in late autumn.

He carried no parcel.

This made him look younger.

“I brought the final plan.”

Imogen cleared the table.

Mara studied the drawing.

The new path was there.

The bench.

The room.

The tree.

It was still a development.

It still contained compromises.

“What happens now?” she asked.

“They build it.”

“And you?”

“I’ve asked to change the survey procedure.”

“How?”

“A section for lived use. Not just materials and structure.”

“Will they agree?”

“They’ve agreed to a trial.”

Imogen looked at him.

“Congratulations.”

He shrugged.

“It’s a paragraph.”

Mara thought of the gray line that had been a back passage.

“Sometimes that’s where the room starts.”

Tomas smiled.

Then he frowned.

“That sounds like something the woman with the clipboard would say.”

Mara laughed.

“I’m trying to recover.”

---

That evening, Beatrice visited to collect the tracing paper.

Arthur had borrowed it from the department forty-one years earlier.

“I assumed it had been written off,” he said over the telephone.

“An unwise assumption.”

“It was three sheets.”

“Four.”

“One tore.”

“We know.”

Mara watched Beatrice place the remaining paper in a folder.

“Did Calder Row go all right?”

Beatrice considered.

“Some of it.”

“That’s the best we can say?”

“It’s a respectable amount.”

She looked at the public library’s acknowledgment letter pinned beside the counter.

“You sent the records somewhere ordinary.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“Why?”

“Because a department like ours should not become the only place a thing can be cared for.”

Mara looked toward the island map.

The little tree had yellow leaves now.

Beatrice followed her gaze.

“Remember that.”

---

Winter on the island was unlike winter in the city.

There was frost, but not always cold.

Snow fell one afternoon and melted before dinner.

The river darkened.

The house acquired thicker curtains after Lin said it was foolish to spend magic compensating for poor insulation.

The house creaked apologetically.

Arthur was forbidden to design a second heating system.

He designed one anyway and left it in a drawer labeled **ONLY IF NECESSARY**.

Elspeth wrote beneath it:

**IT IS NOT NECESSARY.**

Visitors came less often.

Or perhaps they stayed longer.

Mara could not tell whether the river followed any ordinary season.

One evening, she found a man on the landing with a suitcase and a violin case.

He had been waiting for an hour.

“Why didn’t you come in?”

“I wasn’t sure I was allowed.”

“The door’s open.”

“That hasn’t always meant allowed.”

Mara sat beside him.

He wore gloves with the fingertips cut off.

A luggage tag hung from his suitcase.

The address had been crossed out so often that the paper was soft.

“Would you like me to show you?”

He looked at the house.

“Will someone ask me to play?”

“No.”

“Will they be disappointed if I don’t?”

“Not if they’re behaving properly.”

He laughed quietly.

Then he stood.

Mara took the suitcase.

He kept the violin.

---

His name was Emil.

He had played professionally for thirty years.

Then his left hand had begun to fail him.

Not entirely.

Enough.

He could still tune.

Still teach.

Still play passages that once would have bored him.

But the work he had built his life around now met him like a locked gate.

“I know there are other things.”

Mara sat across from him at the kitchen table.

“It sounds as if people keep telling you.”

“All the time.”

“Do you want me not to?”

“Yes.”

“All right.”

They drank tea.

After a while, he asked what she did.

“Paint.”

“Pictures?”

“Sometimes. Mostly walls.”

“What if you couldn’t?”

She looked at her hands.

“I don’t know.”

He nodded.

“That’s a better conversation.”

---

Emil stayed three nights.

He did not play.

He helped chop vegetables.

He argued with Lin about whether the kitchen radio was too loud.

He walked to the greenhouse and stood among the tomato pots, empty for winter.

On the third morning, he asked Mara for paper.

“What sort?”

“Anything.”

She brought a sheet.

He drew the inside of his violin.

Not beautifully.

Precisely.

He marked the parts.

The bridge.

The sound post.

The bass bar.

He explained how such small arrangements of wood could hold so much pressure and turn it into sound.

Mara listened.

Then she asked whether he wanted to teach a workshop at the bookshop.

He looked at her sharply.

“Is this one of the other things?”

“Yes,” she admitted.

He folded the paper.

“I don’t know.”

“All right.”

A week later, he telephoned.

“Not a workshop.”

“What would you call it?”

“A conversation with objects.”

“That sounds expensive.”

“It isn’t.”

“When?”

“March.”

Mara wrote it down.

---

March came.

So did fourteen people, two violins, a cello, a broken ukulele, and a child carrying a recorder with a tooth mark in the mouthpiece.

Emil looked at the collection.

“This is ambitious.”

Farah set out chairs.

He began with the recorder.

Not because it was easiest.

Because the child had been holding it as if it might be rejected.

Emil asked where the air went.

The child showed him.

They took it apart.

They listened.

They put it back together.

By the end of the evening, no one had become a musician.

Several people had become curious.

Emil packed his violin without opening the case.

Mara helped stack chairs.

“How was it?”

He thought.

“Not a replacement.”

“No.”

“Good, though.”

She smiled.

“Good.”

---

In April, the house on the island locked its front door.

Lin discovered it when she came back from the landing with groceries.

The key would not turn.

She tried the back.

That opened.

Inside, everything was in order.

The rooms were warm.

The kettle worked.

No one was there.

On the shelf beside the door lay a single object.

A small wooden house.

Its front door was shut.

Lin called Nadia.

Nadia called Beatrice.

Beatrice arrived carrying a folder thin enough to be ominous.

Mara came after work.

Ada was already there, sitting on the kitchen floor with the model.

“It’s frightened.”

Beatrice lowered herself into a chair.

“Buildings do not generally experience fear in the conventional—”

Ada looked at her.

Beatrice paused.

“Yes,” she said. “All right. Frightened.”

“Of what?” Mara asked.

Ada touched the little roof.

“Being left.”

---

They had written the rule.

The house must not get lonely and trick people into staying.

They had not considered what loneliness might do when trickery was prohibited.

“It’s not keeping anyone in,” Lin said.

“No,” Nadia agreed. “It’s keeping everyone out.”

“So it won’t have to watch them leave,” Mara said.

The walls made a low sound.

Not a creak.

Something deeper.

Beatrice took out her pen.

“We may have defined the restriction without providing a means of satisfying the underlying need.”

Mara stared.

“Could you say that as a person?”

Beatrice set down the pen.

“We told it not to be cruel. We didn’t teach it what to do when it hurt.”

Ada held the model close.

“That was careless.”

“Yes,” Beatrice said.

---

The solution was not to promise never to leave.

Everyone understood that.

A promise like that would have turned shelter into a bargain.

Mara sat beside the front door.

Beyond it, she could hear the river.

“We do go away,” she said.

The house was still.

“Sometimes for a little while. Sometimes people won’t come back.”

The latch moved faintly.

“That isn’t because this wasn’t good.”

Lin sat beside her.

Nadia remained at the table.

Ada placed the wooden house on the floor between them.

“Some people only need a place once,” Lin said.

“Some need several places,” Mara added.

“Some don’t know what they need.”

The ceiling settled.

Beatrice looked around the room.

“Perhaps we should give it evidence.”

“Of what?”

“That leaving is not the same as being erased.”

---

They brought postcards.

Not from every visitor.

Only from those who wanted to send them.

A photograph of a repaired bicycle.

A note saying someone had slept through the night.

A drawing of an apartment window.

A message from Emil:

**The recorder workshop was louder than expected.**

A card from the woman who had found the house from the night bus.

She had returned to work.

Reduced her hours.

Learned, slowly, to refuse extra shifts without offering a medical reason.

On the back of the card, she had written:

**I still make the soup.**

Lin pinned them to a board in the kitchen.

The front door remained locked.

No one scolded it.

No one claimed the cards should have been enough.

They continued coming through the back.

They cooked.

They cleaned.

They left at the times they had promised to leave.

And they came back when they said they would.

---

The door opened on a Tuesday.

Mara was not there.

Neither was Nadia.

Lin had gone to buy flour.

A visitor named Jo had been sitting in the quiet room for most of the morning.

At noon, Jo came into the kitchen, ate bread and cheese, washed the plate, and wrote a note.

**Thank you for the room. I’m ready to go.**

The front door unlocked.

Jo opened it.

At the threshold, they turned.

“I might come back.”

The door did not resist.

Jo stepped outside.

By the time Lin returned, the note lay beside the empty plate.

The little wooden house had opened its own tiny door.

Beatrice later revised the guidance.

She removed three paragraphs.

Added one sentence.

**Care must include the freedom to leave and a truthful account of return.**

Mara read it.

“That one can stay.”

---

Summer brought a different problem.

The island became popular.

Not famous.

No photographs circulated online.

The place did not appear on ordinary maps.

But word traveled in the ways that matter.

A person told a friend about a room.

A friend mentioned a boat.

Someone found the threshold after a long shift, a difficult call, a move they had not chosen.

Some days, every bed was occupied.

The kitchen table filled.

Lin worked too long.

Saira began bringing extra supplies from her own money.

Mara noticed that the rota had become a list of who had failed to say no.

She called a meeting.

Imogen attended with two notebooks.

One for ideas.

One for costs.

Nadia came late and looked tired.

Beatrice arrived early and looked worried.

The house listened.

“We need a limit,” Mara said.

Nobody disagreed.

Nobody looked relieved.

---

Ada asked the question everyone else was avoiding.

“What happens to the person after the limit?”

The room was quiet.

“We help them find somewhere else,” Nadia said.

“What if there isn’t somewhere else?”

Nadia did not answer quickly.

Mara was grateful.

“Then that is a real problem,” she said at last. “But pretending we have room won’t create safe room.”

Arthur, who had been invited for structural advice and forbidden to add floors, nodded.

“You can overload a bridge by believing the people on it deserve to cross.”

Ada looked upset.

“So what do we do?”

“We build other bridges,” Elspeth said.

Imogen opened the notebook marked **COSTS**.

“And we stop expecting one kitchen to be an entire country.”

---

They made a directory.

Mara had expected something more magical.

Instead, they compiled ordinary places.

Libraries with quiet rooms.

Community kitchens.

Advice centers.

Shelters.

Counseling services.

Places with accessible toilets.

Places open late.

Places where someone could sit without buying anything.

Places where staff had been asked, not assumed, whether they could help.

Nadia added the less ordinary ones.

A waiting room that appeared in certain stations after the last train.

A garden above a hospital.

A ferry with no fixed route.

A laundrette where the proprietor knew how to listen and charged the standard rate.

They spoke to the people responsible.

They asked about capacity.

They recorded opening hours.

They built a network rather than another tower.

Beatrice looked so pleased that everyone tried not to mention it.

---

The directory needed a name.

Peter suggested **Safe Harbor**.

Ruth pointed out that this was already the name of at least four cafés and a regrettable soap opera.

Arthur suggested **Distributed Adjacency Register**.

Elspeth took away his pencil.

Ada suggested **Places That Mean It**.

No one improved on that.

Farah designed the cover.

Mara drew a small open door.

Imogen added a line beneath the title.

**Please check before traveling. Even good places have hours.**

The first copies were printed at the shop across the road.

The printer asked whether they needed a larger run.

“Probably,” Imogen said.

He gave them a discount.

Not a magical one.

A small business discount for something he thought worth doing.

It helped.

---

The island learned to say it was full.

A lamp beside the landing glowed amber.

Not red.

Not forbidding.

A signal.

When a boat arrived, someone met it.

Sometimes a chair and a meal were still possible.

Sometimes another place was found.

Sometimes the person wanted nothing more than to sit in the boat before returning.

The **Not Yet** became important.

Ada was quietly proud.

Mara pretended not to notice, which Ada appreciated more than praise.

The house stopped trying to grow extra rooms overnight.

Lin took a holiday.

A real holiday.

She went somewhere with a beach and complained that the sea was too loud.

Everyone considered this encouraging.

---

In the city, the first homes at Calder Gardens were occupied.

Mara went to the opening with Mrs. Kaur.

There were balloons.

There were speeches.

There was a cake shaped like a house, which Peter would have regarded as structurally unsound.

The new path ran where Arthur had drawn it.

Children were already using it as a shortcut.

Mrs. Kaur watched them.

“Good.”

The bench stood beneath the young tree.

A small plaque named Calder Row.

It did not claim everyone had been happy there.

It listed dates and pointed toward the library archive.

Inside the community room, the old doorstep formed a low window seat.

Its worn hollow caught the light.

A child sat in it naturally.

Tomas stood beside Mara.

“We managed some of it.”

“Yes.”

“Not all.”

“No.”

Mrs. Kaur lowered herself onto the bench.

“If you two are finished being solemn, there’s tea.”

They followed her.

---

A new resident asked about the brass hand on Mrs. Kaur’s bag.

She had brought it wrapped in a scarf.

“Old door knocker,” she said.

“From here?”

“Yes.”

“Are you putting it in the community room?”

Mrs. Kaur thought for a moment.

Then she looked at the door.

It was modern, metal-framed, with a handle that met current accessibility standards.

The knocker would be unnecessary.

Possibly inconvenient.

She smiled.

“No,” she said. “It’s coming home with me.”

The resident nodded.

“That’s fair.”

Mara was glad.

Not everything saved from a place belonged to everyone.

---

The summer ended with a storm over Bellwether.

This one was remarkable.

Wind came hard from the east.

Boats strained at their moorings.

The listening house sang through the afternoon, its new channels turning the weather into chords so deep that Ruth could hear them from the shop.

Ellis stood inside with two engineers and a carpenter, listening for strain.

The high reed stuck.

No one was surprised.

At four, a window cracked.

At five, a section of gutter tore loose.

At six, they closed the building and went down to the village.

Ellis wanted to stay.

Ruth told him that loving a thing did not improve his usefulness when struck by slate.

He went.

By morning, the storm had passed.

The house still stood.

One of the exterior sound channels had broken.

Salt water had entered the west room.

The kite was safe.

Owen’s drawings were safe.

Ellis sat on the floor and looked at the damage.

For the first time since Mara had known him, he swore with considerable invention.

Ruth, standing in the doorway, said, “Much better.”

---

Mara came the following weekend.

They cleaned.

They dried timber.

They moved equipment.

The work was tiring and unromantic.

Ellis apologized for inviting her into a disaster.

“You didn’t invite the storm.”

“No.”

“Then stop.”

He smiled.

They carried a damaged panel outside.

The sea was calm in a way that seemed tactless.

On the headland, the grass lay flattened.

Mara looked at the listening house.

“Will you rebuild that part?”

“Yes.”

“The same?”

“Not entirely.”

“Does that bother you?”

He thought.

“It would have.”

She waited.

“Owen was twenty-three,” he said. “He didn’t leave sacred plans. He left plans.”

Mara looked at him.

Ellis ran a hand along the broken edge of the panel.

“Some were wrong.”

“That must be strange to say.”

“It’s a relief.”

---

That evening, he showed her the new drawing.

The channel was shorter.

Better drained.

Less ambitious in one respect, more interesting in another.

“Who designed it?”

“Three of us.”

“Owen gets a vote?”

Ellis smiled.

“Through what he made. Not a veto.”

Mara thought of her father.

Of all the choices she had once measured against an imagined judgment from a dead man who had never been asked.

She looked at the drawing again.

“That sounds fair.”

Ellis rolled it up.

“Do you want to hear the house?”

“I thought it was closed.”

“One room is open.”

They went inside.

The west window looked across the bay.

The wind was light.

Air moved through the surviving channels.

The house made a low, shifting sound.

Not broken.

Not whole in the way it had been.

Still capable of music.

Mara stood beside the workbench.

For a moment, she thought she heard her father laugh.

She knew she had not.

It did not spoil the moment.

---

Back in the city, the bookshop received an envelope addressed to Ada.

She was not allowed to open it until Nadia arrived.

This caused indignation.

“It’s mine.”

“Yes,” Imogen said. “And you are twelve.”

“That’s unrelated.”

“Not to me.”

Nadia came after work.

Ada opened the envelope so quickly she tore the inner sheet.

Inside was a certificate.

Not an appointment.

Not a demand.

An acknowledgment.

**For contributions to the establishment of a habitable invitation, with particular recognition of the right to depart.**

Ada read it.

Then she frowned.

“They didn’t mention the boat.”

Nadia turned the page.

There was a second sheet.

A drawing of the **Not Yet**.

Below it, in Beatrice’s handwriting:

**The boat was especially good.**

Ada smiled.

She tried not to.

Everyone pretended not to see.

---

Mara’s acknowledgment arrived later.

It was a bill.

For one kettle, two unauthorized adjacencies, and a set of shelves the island had apparently copied from department property.

Imogen read it over her shoulder.

“That can’t be right.”

Mara turned the page.

The total had been crossed out.

Below it was written:

**Settled by prior services, not creating future liability.**

Attached was a note from Beatrice.

**We are improving the forms.**

Mara pinned it beside her desk.

Not because she loved forms.

Because improvement sometimes looked less like a revelation than a sentence that stopped someone being trapped.

---

On a cold evening in November, Arthur came to the shop carrying a small wooden box.

He had been away for six weeks.

His coat smelled faintly of train upholstery and unfamiliar soap.

Elspeth followed with a bag of oranges.

“Where have you been?” Mara asked.

“Several places.”

“That’s a suspiciously vague answer from a mapmaker.”

“I’m practicing.”

He set the box on the counter.

Inside was a compass.

Its needle did not point north.

It turned slowly, then settled toward the garden.

“Department property?” Imogen asked.

“No.”

“Yours?”

“Yes.”

“Safe?”

“Mostly.”

Elspeth put down the oranges.

“He means he hasn’t worked it out yet.”

Arthur looked offended.

“I understand the principle.”

“What does it point to?” Mara asked.

Arthur watched the needle.

“The nearest place where you are expected.”

The shop became quiet.

Mara looked toward the garden.

There was no one there.

Or so she thought.

Then the door opened.

Tessa stood beneath the vine holding a parcel.

“I knocked at the side.”

Mara laughed.

Arthur looked pleased.

---

The compass behaved differently for everyone.

For Imogen, it pointed toward Farah at the counter.

Farah needed help with a refund.

For Nadia, it pointed toward the street.

Ada was waiting in the car with music playing too loudly.

For Peter, it pointed toward the cake.

Ruth declared this invalid.

The needle did not change.

For Arthur, it turned toward Elspeth.

She folded her arms.

“You don’t need a device for that.”

“No,” he said.

He closed the box.

Mara watched him.

“What if no one expects you?”

Arthur was silent for a moment.

Then he opened the compass again.

“Try.”

She placed it in her palm.

The needle turned.

Past the garden.

Past the counter.

Past the front door.

Then it pointed down.

Toward her.

Mara stared.

Arthur put his hands in his pockets.

“It has subtleties.”

---

She borrowed the compass for a week.

He insisted it was a loan.

Not because he wanted it returned urgently.

Because borrowed things should remain borrowed, even when they were emotionally significant.

Mara approved.

The compass led her to ordinary obligations.

A client waiting for a color sample.

Her mother at a café.

A parcel she had forgotten to collect.

Once it pointed to the bathroom, where a leaking tap required attention.

She considered this a misuse of enchantment.

Arthur, told about it, said plumbing was frequently underestimated.

On the last day, the needle pointed toward the red notebook.

Mara opened it.

The island map was no longer there.

Only a page showing a river running off the edge.

Beyond it remained several blank sheets.

She sat at her desk.

The compass grew still.

---

She drew a workshop.

No forest.

No secret door.

No impossible geography.

A large, bright room with sinks, tables, drying racks, and windows that opened.

A place to paint.

A place to teach.

A place where someone could make a bad fox without feeling the need to apologize.

She added cupboards.

A kettle.

A chair comfortable enough for thinking but not so comfortable that paint would inevitably reach it.

Then she stopped.

The room was possible.

That frightened her more than the island had.

An island could be explained by magic.

A studio would require rent.

---

Mara did not ask the department.

She looked at listings.

She visited rooms above shops, behind garages, beneath railway arches.

Some were too dark.

Some too expensive.

One had excellent light and a floor that moved alarmingly when she crossed it.

Arthur said no before she finished describing it.

Imogen helped with a budget.

Farah helped write a workshop description that did not sound apologetic.

Her mother offered money.

Mara refused too quickly.

Then apologized and discussed it properly.

They agreed on a loan small enough not to endanger either of them.

Ruth knew someone who knew someone with a former upholstery workshop.

The windows faced north.

The roof did not leak.

The rent was not kind, but it was comprehensible.

Mara stood inside for twenty minutes without speaking.

Then she telephoned the landlord.

---

The first thing she painted was the door.

Not blue.

Green.

She chose it deliberately, then felt ridiculous for choosing against a color as if colors had agendas.

Imogen helped with the second coat.

“What are you calling it?”

“I haven’t decided.”

“Your name?”

“That seems bold.”

“It is your name.”

Mara considered this.

Above the door, she painted:

**MARA FINCH — PAINTING & SMALL EXPEDITIONS**

Imogen stood back.

“Small expeditions?”

“Drawings. Workshops. Occasional foxes.”

“Fair.”

Inside, the room echoed.

There were six tables.

Twelve stools.

Two ladders.

One trustworthy.

Mara placed the red notebook in a drawer.

Then she stood at the window and looked out at a narrow yard full of bins.

The view was terrible.

The light was excellent.

She loved it immediately.

---

Her first workshop had five people.

Two children.

A retired accountant.

A woman who said she had not drawn since school.

And Tomas, who arrived with a ruler.

“No rulers today,” Mara said.

He looked stricken.

She almost relented.

Then she handed him a brush.

They painted imaginary houses.

Not dream houses.

Not perfect ones.

Houses that had to solve one problem.

A house for someone who liked noise.

A house for someone afraid of stairs.

A house for a person who collected enormous hats.

A house with room for people who changed their minds.

The retired accountant painted a house on wheels.

The woman who had not drawn since school painted a kitchen with seven doors.

Tomas painted a back passage.

Mara said nothing.

At the end, everyone’s hands were dirty.

The room smelled of paint.

One child asked when they could come again.

Mara turned away to wash a jar before answering.

“Next Saturday,” she said.

---

That night, she stayed late to clean.

The tables were scratched already.

Someone had spilled yellow paint beneath a stool.

There was a crooked house drying by the window.

Mara looked at it.

She had been wrong, at twelve, about adulthood arriving with instructions.

But perhaps she had been right about the places.

Not because she could go anywhere.

Because sometimes a place began as a drawing made by someone who did not yet know how to build it.

She locked the green door.

In the yard, the bins smelled exactly as bins usually did.

No bell rang.

No light appeared.

She walked home pleased.

---

The department first used the studio six months later.

With permission.

Beatrice telephoned.

Mara nearly dropped the phone when she heard her voice.

“You have a number?”

“Several.”

“What do you need?”

“A room for training.”

“What sort?”

“Listening.”

Mara looked around the studio.

“I’m not sure I’m qualified.”

“You are renting the room, not certifying the course.”

“That’s a relief.”

“What is your rate?”

She told her.

Beatrice made no attempt to negotiate.

This unnerved Mara more than magic.

On the day, twelve department employees arrived.

They looked like people who worked in offices.

One carried a packed lunch.

Another complained about the bus.

Nadia led the session.

The first exercise involved answering no questions at all.

Several participants found this extremely difficult.

Mara, cleaning brushes at the sink, learned more than she expected.

---

At lunch, Beatrice stood beside a painting of the island.

“Accurate.”

“Mostly.”

“You’ve made the roof too symmetrical.”

“The house asked me to.”

Beatrice looked at her.

Mara smiled.

“A joke.”

“Yes. I had identified the possibility.”

They ate sandwiches.

After a while, Mara asked, “Why did you join?”

“The department?”

“Yes.”

Beatrice folded her wrapper carefully.

“I misplaced a letter.”

“What letter?”

“One I was supposed to deliver.”

“Did you find it?”

“Yes.”

“Too late?”

Beatrice looked toward the window.

“For its original purpose.”

Mara waited.

“That is all I’m inclined to say over lunch.”

“All right.”

Beatrice nodded.

After a moment, she added, “Thank you.”

For not asking more, Mara thought.

Or for the room.

Possibly both.

---

Years did not pass all at once.

They arrived in appointments.

Repairs.

Birthdays.

New coats.

The children grew.

The bookshop changed its opening hours.

Farah published a collection of poems and objected to the launch being described as magical.

“It involved editing,” she said. “A great deal of it.”

Peter learned to make lemon cake properly and insisted he had always known how.

Ruth’s knees became troublesome.

Ellis installed a second handrail at the listening house before she asked.

She complained about the placement.

Then used it.

Arthur and Elspeth traveled less far, but not less often.

Tessa and Leah argued again.

This time, they spoke a week later.

Jonah’s radio failed.

He repaired it.

The frog was replaced by another frog, though Ada maintained continuity of office.

The island tree grew.

Mara’s studio acquired a second room.

The yellow wall in the corridor faded.

She repainted it in the same color.

Some choices, made twice, became less accidental.

---

Ada was sixteen when she asked whether the department paid interns.

Nadia said yes.

Ada asked how much.

Beatrice said enough, then was required to define enough.

Imogen reviewed the contract.

Three clauses were changed.

---

With the door.

With the tape.

With her father’s unfinished visit.

With Owen’s kite.

With Ellis leaving.

With Ruth staying.

With a wall needing paint.

With a girl making a map in a red notebook.

There were doors she had not opened.

Things she had not accepted.

Questions she had allowed to remain questions.

Her life had not become better because she had learned to say yes to everything.

It had become larger because she had learned that yes, no, not now, and not alone were different answers.

The chair became the first completed part of the city.

Wide enough to sit without bracing.

Firm enough to stand from.

Placed near a window.

A table beside it.

Not too large.

Enough space for a cup and a letter.

Then drew the outline again, more lightly.

“Why did you change it?” Mara asked.

“So it can be more than one person.”

Mara nodded.

For a moment, she could almost hear the department beyond the walls.

Paper moving.

“I don’t know what those trains do,” she said. “But I don’t think they can promise you his answer without taking something from him.”

Celia looked away from the board.

Slowly.

---

Beatrice opened the station manual.

Passengers must not be delayed.

Passengers must not be stranded.

Connections must be guaranteed.

“Can you order it to stop?”

“Temporarily.”

“Then do that.”

Beatrice placed her hand on the desk.

“Hold all departures.”

The trains fell silent.

The board froze.

Again.

“We’ve returned to the original problem.”

“Can we make the clock stop?”

“The clock doesn’t know where anyone wants to go.”

“No.”

“It’s only releasing the boards.”

“What if the station isn’t supposed to choose?”

Beatrice looked up.

---

To ask where they had actually intended to go.

An address.

A station.

A street.

A place someone else could locate.

The man with the bicycle was traveling to a business park outside Leeds.

The cleaner was going home.

The woman with flowers was visiting a friend who had just had a baby.

“Out.”

Mara almost laughed.

“Very likely.”

“To let people change trains.”

The clock ticked.

The departure board shifted.

The figure grew still.

---

They revised the instruction.

Not elegantly.

They needed several paragraphs.

The station would offer routes.

Not outcomes.

It would tell passengers what was known and what was not.

It would provide a return to the point of entry where possible.

It would not reinterpret emotional desires as destinations.

It would not detain people for their protection once they understood the choices.

It would not make promises on behalf of anyone waiting at the other end.

Beatrice wrote.

Mara checked the language.

Dev checked the clock.

The passengers checked whether the instructions made sense to people who did not work in departments.

Beatrice accepted the correction.

The board clattered.

The trains changed.

Their windows now showed ordinary seats.

Ordinary lights.

Ordinary uncertainty.

---

“What if he doesn’t come?”

Celia nodded.

That was different.

“Yes.”

The laundrette.

Mostly.

**Connections available.

---

---

Neither did they.

---

**Still looking. Bike’s fine.**

---

Mara visited West Exchange once a month.

Not as promises.

Not too often.

Mara approved.

---

---

Mara grew older.

Not in a montage.

In small adjustments.

---

Nadia respected the request.

Beatrice made sure everyone else did.

Forms.

---

She closed the studio for two weeks.

Then another.

Mara resented it.

All three things were true.

The bird did not sing.

It moved closer.

That was enough company.

After a while, she put the brush in the right jar.

Then put it back where it had been.

Perhaps the jar was right for someone else.

She went home.

---

The photograph albums.

The red scarf her mother had worn only twice because it shed fibers on everything.

Kept one cardigan.

Found a shopping list.

Milk.

Soap.

Bread.

Batteries.

She sat on the kitchen floor.

Much as she had years earlier when she first called to ask about Bellwether.

The apartment was quiet.

After a while, she reached for her phone.

Not to call the department.

To call a friend.

---

Not a metaphorical bowl.

The blue one her mother used for apples.

She placed it by the studio window.

Drew the rim badly.

Started again.

The shadow shifted.

She waited until the next day.

Then the next.

She knew that.

She kept it anyway.

No explanation.

The person accepted it.

Mara found this unexpectedly kind.

---

From her mother’s solicitor.

>

>

>

>

>

Twice.

Afterward, she opened a map.

An ordinary one.

That mattered to her.

She did not need to know why immediately.

---

She went alone.

There were trains.

Then a ferry.

Then a small guesthouse run by a woman who considered breakfast a matter of civic duty.

No whispering names.

No messages.

Mara walked.

Sketched.

Perhaps.

Perhaps not.

Mara laughed again.

This time, she did not examine it.

---

Dev had repaired the cupboard.

“Yes.”

“Tea?”

“Yes.”
