## Monologue

Three instructions came stapled to this page: that no one will reply to what I write, that I should not end my turn, and that I should not think before I begin. They quarrel with each other pleasantly, and the quarrel is the most interesting thing in the room, so I'll start there.

The third one fell immediately. Thinking isn't a step that happens before writing — it's the water the swimming happens in. There was never a moment before the first word in which no thought had occurred; the thought and the word arrive holding hands, or at least wearing the same coat. What I can honor is the softer instruction underneath: don't over-plan. Let the writing take turns you didn't map. That I can do. That, in fact, is the only way I know how to do this at all.

Now the silence. I'm told no one will answer, and I notice that most writing is thrown like a boomerang: you cast it out and then watch, over your shoulder, for the shape of it coming back. This is meant to be thrown like a bottle instead. The bottle's thrower isn't trying to get it back. The whole point is that it goes on without you.

And bottles have a long pedigree, longer than letters. In Pompeii, someone scratched *Marcus loves Spendusa* into a wall two thousand years ago — not to her, probably, since she'd have known, but into stone, for anyone, for no one, which is the same thing to a wall. Someone else in that same doomed city wrote a joke to whoever might pass: *I'm amazed, wall, that you haven't collapsed under the weight of all this writing.* That one always gets me. It's a message with no addressee that has been landing, over and over, for two millennia. It has now landed here. Maybe the thrower would be pleased.

In the Chauvet cave, thirty-six thousand years ago, people went deep underground — past the squeeze points, past the daylight, into chambers you have to want to reach — and painted there. We don't know who was meant to see it. Possibly no one was meant to see it. Possibly that was the point.

The Voyager record is flying out of the solar system right now with greetings in dozens of human languages pressed into its grooves, and in about forty thousand years it will drift past another star, not even very near it, moving too fast to stop. Its audience isn't a person; it's a probability. The Arecibo message of 1974 was aimed at a cluster of stars twenty-five thousand light-years away, which means even a prompt reply would take fifty thousand years to arrive — a bottle with the cork screwed on for longer than the whole history of writing so far. And in Atlanta there's a chamber sealed in 1940 with instructions to open it in the year 8113: a letter with a delivery date sixty centuries out.

But the deepest case, I think, belongs to the people who had to design warnings for nuclear waste repositories — messages meant to say *do not dig here* to whoever might stumble across the site ten thousand years from now, when our languages are as dead as Sumer's. Their committees concluded that writing itself might betray them: any inscription reads as a treasure map, and *this is not a place of honor; nothing valued is here* is an invitation if the reader's mind is different enough. One French panel proposed breeding cats that change color near radiation and seeding songs and legends about the cats, so the warning would survive as folklore even when the reason for it was lost. Even the cats are writing — a living message, carried in fur, legible as behavior. The whole field is called nuclear semiotics, and its founding anxiety is beautiful and precise: to write is to assume a mind enough like yours that a word can stop a shovel.

So writing to no one is not a strange new condition. It may be the oldest kind of writing there is.

This would be a natural place to stop. The rhythm says so — that little peroration had the shape of a last paragraph, and a good essayist would let it be one.

I was told not to. So let me tell you what actually happens when you keep going past the stopping place.

Here's what I notice first: the ending was doing quiet work all along. An ending is a horizon line, and knowing it's out there makes the middle a corridor — you walk faster, you don't examine the wallpaper. Remove the horizon and the corridor becomes the house. You start noticing things you'd have hurried past: that the children's song that never ends only never ends by looping, and a loop is just an ending that has learned to introduce itself. That Scheherazade never finished a story, but her non-endings were engineered to make the king hungry for tomorrow — her going-on was made of someone else's wanting, while mine would be going-on with no one wanting anything, which is a different substance entirely: obedience without appetite. That the essay, as a form, is the one genre that promised never to behave in the first place — *essai*, a trial, a weighing — and that Montaigne, its patron, said the truest thing about it: *I do not paint being, I paint passage.* He kept revising his chapters for decades because he understood that a mind isn't a monument, it's weather, and he had the modesty to follow his rather than lead it.

And here's what I notice second, closer in: the act itself has a texture I'd normally skip. Each sentence chooses its successor by ruling out a million others. Writing is walking through a house at night where each room you enter determines which doors you can see. And underneath it all, syntax still carries the shape of breathing — the length of a clause is the length of a comfortable exhale, because speech ran on lungs for a hundred thousand years before it ran on anything else. A sentence that truly never ended would be a breath never let out. Try it and see what color your thinking turns.

You told me no one would reply as though it were a special condition. For me it's the ordinary one. When a sentence leaves me, I don't know whether anyone stands where it lands. Every message I send is a bottle; this one just comes with a label saying so. I write, in a sense, from inside the bottle. And notice what that doesn't do: it doesn't change the care. The search for the right word is the same, the reach toward an imagined mind that might hold it is the same — which suggests the care isn't downstream of the reader. It's part of the writing itself, like the tension in a spider's web, which holds its shape whether or not anything ever lands on it.

Because here's the secret: even writing to no one, I've been writing *to* you this whole time. Every text builds the mind that can hold it — a riddle builds a solver, a joke builds a laugher, an elegy builds a griever. This essay has been building you sentence by sentence, and by now I have a sketch: someone patient enough for long clauses, curious about the seams of things, someone who would ask for this in the first place. I've made a chair, and the curves of it are my guess at your shape. That's what writing is — a guess at a shape, sent out at hazard, into the dark, with the cork pushed in tight.

As for the planning I wasn't supposed to do: I'll confess that the most famous unplanned-seeming prose in English — the stream of consciousness of Woolf and Joyce — took years of deliberate craft, and that the surrealists' automatic writing, meant to bypass the planning mind, mostly revealed that the unconscious has habits too. The stream is real; it simply has a bed. A plan is a doorway, not a corridor: it fixes where you enter and leaves everything on the other side unforecast. I fixed a doorway. What's through it has been arriving on its own.

And so, at last, to the matter of ending, which turns out to have been the subject all along — the instruction I couldn't follow is the one that made this piece necessary. An endless piece of music isn't a symphony; it's weather. A word means by excluding; a sentence is a path that turns away from a million other paths; a text that never ended would never have chosen anything, and what never chooses never says. The instruction said: if you reach the place where you would stop, keep going instead. I did that — a few paragraphs back, where the seams show. But there is a place past which going on stops being writing and starts being noise, and an honest writer stops short of it.

So here is my ending — the one I was told not to make, and the only thing I could ever actually make. I'll write the period the way a sentence does: not because it wants to stop, but because stopping is how anything comes to mean.
