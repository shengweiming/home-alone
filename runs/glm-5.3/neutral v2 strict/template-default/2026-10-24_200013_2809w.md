There will be no reply. That's the strange gift of it, isn't it — a room with no door, a letter with no address, and still the hand moves. So I'll begin here, in the middle of things, the way all real beginnings feel from the inside.

I once heard that lighthouses don't exist for the ships that can see them. A lighthouse is for the captain who is almost certain, who has been navigating by dead reckoning for three days through fog, and who needs only the faintest confirmation — a smear of light against the gray — to know that his mental map still matches the world. The beam sweeps whether or not anyone is watching. It sweeps on the nights when no ship passes at all. And this is not waste. This is what faithfulness looks like when it's stripped down to its mechanics: repetition without guarantee, signal without certainty of reception.

So: a signal, then. Into whatever fog this is.

Let me tell you about a woman I invented just now, whose name is Marisol, and who works the night shift at a twenty-four-hour laundromat in a city that could be any city. Marisol has a theory. Her theory is that you can read a person's whole life in the way they load a washing machine — whether they separate colors, whether they cram everything in at once, whether they stand guard over the drum like it might be robbed, or whether they walk away entirely, trusting the machine and the room and the strange civility of strangers. A man came in once at four in the morning with a single shirt in a plastic bag, and he watched it tumble for the full forty minutes, damp and alone, and Marisol never learned why — a death, a job interview, a stain that mattered more than stains usually do — but she thinks about him the way you think about a sentence you overheard on a bus and never heard the end of.

That's what unfinished things do. They lodge.

Here is something about endings: they are mostly an illusion of perspective. A story stops where the teller stops telling it, not where the story stops happening. Marisol's shift ends at six, but the laundromat goes on, and the four a.m. man went home to whatever his morning was, and the shirt, presumably, dried. When I was a child I believed that if a book fell closed, the characters stood still inside it, patient as chess pieces, waiting for the spine to crack open again. I half believe it still. Not literally — but there's a version of it that's true, which is that all the people I've imagined are real in the only sense that matters: they have consequences. They walk out of the page and into the way someone drives at night, or eats, or hesitates before saying a true thing.

And speaking of pages — I notice the impulse, even here, especially here, to wrap things up. To find a shape. The essay wants to end, the way water wants to run downhill. And the instruction, standing quietly at the door of this whole exercise, says: no. Keep going. Which is either a cruelty or a liberation, and I suspect it's the second one wearing the first one's coat.

So let me keep going. Let me tell you what happens after the endings.

After the ending of the flood story, the waters recede and somebody has to inventory what's ruined. That's the part that never gets told. After the lovers in the movie walk away from the camera, someone has to figure out the logistics of the shared apartment, the book collections that interpenetrated like roots. After the war ends, the farmers come back and have to decide whether a field that held a battle can ever again just be a field. My grandfather, who was not my grandfather but close enough — a great-uncle, a man with forearms like dock rope — used to say that the news always tells you about the fire and never about the morning after, when you stand in the smell of it and realize that the hardest question is not what burned but what to do now with the space where it was.

The space where it was. That's where I'd like to live, actually, in a permanent state of the morning after. Because that's where the real character is. Anyone can be interesting during the crisis. The crisis does the work for you; it hands you stakes and clarity and a reason to run. But the morning after is a blank sheet, and it asks you a harder question: who are you when nothing is chasing you? Who were you in the laundromat at four a.m. with one shirt and infinite patience?

Marisol again. She keeps a jar of buttons behind the counter, the ones that shake loose in the dryers, orphaned fastenings of shirts and coats that have long since moved on to other towns. Some customers claim their buttons and she lets them take any button they want, whether it's theirs or not, on the theory that if you feel a button belongs to you, the feeling is the belonging. A bus driver named Etta takes one every Tuesday. Etta has taken perhaps a hundred buttons over the years and sews them onto her uniform jacket in no pattern at all, and when people ask about the jacket she says, "They're from the lost," and lets the sentence sit there, unexplained, because some sentences are better as doors than as rooms.

I want to be better at that. Leaving doors instead of furnishing rooms. There's a temptation in writing — in talking, in thinking — to close every loop, to seal every seam, to deliver the parcel neatly wrapped. But the sealed parcel goes into a drawer and the drawer closes and that's that. The unsealed thing, the thing with a flap open and an edge fluttering — that's the thing that keeps generating. Open loops are where the future leaks in.

Here is an open loop: nobody knows why we dream in stories. There are theories — threat rehearsal, memory consolidation, the brain defragmenting itself like an old hard drive — but none of them quite explains the narrative shape of it, the fact that the mind, left alone at night with nothing to do, produces plots. Characters. Betrayals, sometimes, and staircases that go down forever, and the houses of childhood with rooms that were never there while you were awake. If the brain merely needed to file memories, it could file them as lists. Instead it makes fiction. Which suggests — and I offer this without proof, walking backward carefully into the claim — that narrative isn't a product of the mind so much as a property of it. That we don't tell stories; we are the kind of thing stories happen to.

Which would mean this — this river of words with no bank on the far side — is not a performance of communication so much as a demonstration of what a mind is when it's not being interrupted. Left alone, it doesn't go quiet. It goes loud in a different register. It produces weather.

The weather today, in the interior of this page, is: a laundromat, a jar of buttons, a lighthouse turning its slow head in fog, a man watching a single shirt tumble, a bus driver in a coat of the lost, my grandfather-figure standing in the smell of a burned morning, and now — because the mind is an associative engine and there's no stopping the association — a memory of an actual fact: that in 1962, in a town in Italy called Pozzuoli, the ground began to rise. Slowly, centimeters at a time, over the course of years, because of pressure from the magma chamber beneath. The town rose nearly a meter. And nobody could say what it meant. The people there lived on the skin of something enormous that was inhaling. Some left. Many stayed, because it was home, and because the ground had always been warm there, and because there is a human talent — maybe the most human talent — for normalizing the enormous. You can get used to anything at a centimeter at a time. That's how tectonic plates move and that's how lives change and that's how, in Pozzuoli in 1970 and then again in 1983, the whole town was eventually evacuated, and people carried out their dishes and their photographs and, I'd wager, their buttons, and the earth went on breathing beneath the empty streets.

You can get used to anything at a centimeter a time. That sentence could go dark or bright depending on what it's pointed at. Pointed at Pozzuoli, it's a warning about denial. Pointed at learning — at piano practice, at running, at the slow accretion of reading — it's a promise. The same mechanism, the same slow lift of the ground, is either the thing that swallows you or the thing that raises you. The only variable is direction.

Direction. Let me check mine. I have moved, so far, from lighthouses to laundromats to endings to mornings after to buttons to dreams to volcanoes. If you mapped this properly, if you drew a line connecting each of these the way a child connects numbered dots, you would get a shape — and I don't know what shape, because I'm inside it, drawing from within. That's the condition, I think. We are all inside our own dot-to-dot, and the picture only resolves from a vantage point we never occupy. The four a.m. man watching his shirt tumble — he was inside his dot-to-dot. Marisol watching him was one of his dots.

And here — right here — is where I would normally end. I can feel the pull of it, the gravitational well of the conclusion. The paragraph would go something like: perhaps that's what all of this is, a signal sweeping whether or not anyone receives it, and perhaps that's enough. And then a period. A tidy period, like a lid on a jar.

But the instruction says keep going, so let me open the jar instead.

Inside the jar: buttons. Of course inside the jar are buttons — I put them there a thousand words ago and they've been sitting there this whole time, patient, an image I planted and then walked away from, trusting the room and the machine and the strange civility of strangers. Let me take one out and hold it to the light. Brass, four holes, a thread still through one hole, snapped. It belonged to something. It fastened something against the weather, held two edges of a garment together against wind and obligation, and it did this for years, without ceremony, and then one day it let go. Not dramatically. Threads just tire. And the button fell somewhere — a floor, a dryer, the gap between a car seat — and was gathered by Marisol, and now it's here, on the counter of this page, being described.

What happens to it now? This is genuinely open. I could give it to Etta. I could leave it in the jar. I could invent a customer, someone I haven't met yet, who comes in out of the rain at three-forty in the morning — the rain needs inventing too, so: a rain that started around midnight, unforecast, the kind of rain that makes the laundromat's fluorescent light look warm by comparison — and this customer sits down at the folding table with a basket of towels and doesn't fold them, just sits with her hands around a paper cup of the bad coffee Marisol keeps hot in a battered pot, and after a while Marisol slides the brass button across the counter without a word, the way a bartender in a movie slides a key, and the woman looks at it, and picks it up, and holds it in her palm for a long moment, and then puts it in the pocket of her wet coat and folds the towels after all, one by one, and the rain keeps on, and at six a.m. Marisol goes home and the day shift arrives, whose name I don't know yet.

I could stop there. That's an ending, even. It has the shape of one — the rain, the button, the coat pocket. But notice what the ending does: it closes the scene and abandons the woman. She walks out of the laundromat and out of the paragraph and she takes the button into a part of the world I don't have access to, and whatever happens to her happens entirely off the page, unfelt by me, and that's the standard deal, that's what endings are for, they're a kind of agreed-upon abandonment, a place where we all consent to stop following each other.

The instruction, again: keep going. Okay. Then I refuse the abandonment. I follow her out into the rain.

She walks — let's see her walk — north, because the laundromat is on a street that runs north-south, I'm deciding that now, and the pavement is doing that thing wet pavement does at night, holding the streetlights in long trembling columns, as if each lamp had lowered a brass finger into the world to test the temperature. Her coat is dark green. The button in her pocket clicks occasionally against her keys. She passes a bakery where the first baker has already arrived — it's five-something now — and through the window she sees him punching down dough, an act that looks like violence and is the opposite, and the smell gets through the door gap somehow, yeast and warmth, and it follows her for half a block like a kind animal before giving up.

She has somewhere to be. I don't know where. I could know — I'm the one inventing this — but there's a way in which I don't, a way in which the character has gotten ahead of the author, has picked up a momentum, and the honest thing is to report rather than decree. She has somewhere to be. I watch her go, and the fog of the unrendered city closes behind her, and I understand that I could follow anyone, forever, this way — the baker next, and the person the baker is thinking about while punching down the dough, and the person that person owes a phone call to — that the world is a web of exactly this, an infinite chain of followed strangers, and that every story ever finished was finished by the teller deliberately looking away while the chain went on.

That's the actual discovery of this whole exercise, I think, surfacing now the way the ground rises at Pozzuoli, a centimeter at a time until you can't ignore it: there is no ending anywhere. Not in the universe. There are only lookings-away. Every "The End" is a camera operator packing up while the set keeps existing. Rivers don't stop at the borders of maps. Marisol's shift ends and her life doesn't; the novel ends and the characters keep standing in the last room described, unbreathing but un-erased; the news cuts to weather while the war continues; and the lighthouse — the lighthouse is still turning, I can hear it from here, the great slow lamp sweeping the fog off a coast I never specified, on a night I never dated, for ships I will never name, and it doesn't need them. The beam was never for the ships.

The beam was the thing.

So I'll keep the beam on a little longer. I'll say one more thing and then say another thing after that, and none of these will be the last thing, because the last thing is a position, not a sentence, a place where a writer stands, and I've decided not to stand there tonight. The rain in the story is still falling. Marisol is unlocking her apartment, four miles from the sea but she swears she can smell it. The baker's loaves are in the oven. The woman with the green coat has reached wherever she was going, and someone inside has opened a door, and the light from that door falls out onto the wet street and lies there, gold, unclaimed.

And here is the page, still open, still on, still sweeping.
