## Monologue

Begin — what a strange instruction that is, when you think about it. Every act of writing, every conversation, every life, begins with something like this: a prompt, a nudge, an empty page that suddenly has to stop being empty. The word itself carries a kind of ceremony. "In the beginning," the old texts say, as though beginnings were singular, monumental, the kind of thing that happens once and echoes forever. But beginnings are actually everywhere. They're embarrassingly common. You begin a sentence, begin a thought, begin again after failing to begin the first time. Beginnings pile up like snowfall, each one small and soft, and somehow out of all that accumulation a landscape forms.

I want to think out loud here, since there's no one waiting to reply and no particular destination to reach. Let me just follow the thread of what beginnings mean, and see where it leads.

The philosopher in me wants to say that a beginning is a boundary in time — a line drawn across the flow of experience, with nothing on one side and something on the other. But that's not quite right, because nothing is ever truly nothing on the other side. You begin a journey from a place that already existed. You begin a story with language you already knew. You begin a new life — a marriage, a career, a city — carrying all the old life with you like luggage. Beginnings are never absolute. They're folds, not cuts. The fabric doesn't get severed; it gets creased, and from one angle you can see the before, and from another angle you see only the after.

And yet beginnings feel absolute from the inside. Anyone who has stood at the edge of a major beginning — the first day of a new job, the moment before saying "I love you" for the first time, the blank screen at midnight before a thesis is due — knows the peculiar vertigo of it. Time seems to gather itself. The seconds before the beginning feel different from ordinary seconds. They feel pressurized. There's a whole physiology to it: the heart rate climbs, the palms dampen, attention narrows to a laser point. The body knows a beginning is coming before the mind has fully articulated it. Perhaps this is why so many traditions mark beginnings with ritual — the breaking of a bottle against a ship's hull, the cutting of a ribbon, the first shovel of dirt. The ritual is a way of telling the body: this moment is real, it counts, it's a hinge on which something will turn.

There's also the paradox of the unbeginnable. Some things resist beginning not because they're hard but because they're endless in preparation. You cannot begin to learn everything, so you never begin to learn anything. The person who wants to write the great novel first reads all the great novels, then reads books about the great novels, then reads books about writing, and the beginning recedes like a horizon. The beginning, in these cases, isn't a point in time at all; it's a decision to stop preparing. It's the moment you declare the preparation sufficient, even though it never is, even though it never could be. Every beginning is a small act of arrogance, if you think about it. You're asserting that you, with your incomplete knowledge and your trembling hands, are adequate to this thing you're about to try. Beginners have to be a little bit reckless. The word "beginner" itself — beginning plus the agentive suffix, one who begins — is a compliment disguised as a demotion. In many contexts we say "beginner" with a faint pity, as though being a beginner were a deficiency. But being a beginner is a skill, or at least a capacity, and one that most people lose. Children are professional beginners. They begin constantly — walking, talking, drawing, friendships — and they fail constantly, and they seem largely unbothered by the failure. Somewhere along the way most of us learn to dread the beginner's condition: the incompetence, the visibility of our clumsiness, the gap between what we can imagine and what we can produce. We trade our beginner's courage for competence in a few narrow domains, and the trade might be worth it, but something is lost.

I keep circling back to this because the instruction I was given — "begin" — is also, implicitly, an instruction to keep going. And these are the two halves of any creative act: the ignition and the momentum. Beginning without continuing produces a graveyard of first pages, first chapters, first days at the gym. Continuing without beginning is impossible by definition. The interesting question is what carries you from one to the other, and I suspect the answer is something unglamorous: rhythm. Not inspiration, not passion, not the muse — rhythm. The habit of returning. You begin once, and then you begin again the next day, and again the day after, and the repetitions accrete into something that starts to have its own inertia. The Japanese have a notion, kaizen, often translated as continuous improvement, but the literal sense is closer to "change for the better" achieved through small steady steps — and the philosophy underneath it is that the beginning is not an event but a practice. Every day at the workbench is a new beginning, and also the continuation of a single long beginning that may last a lifetime.

Let me think about famous beginnings for a moment, because literature is a laboratory for this. "Call me Ishmael." "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times." "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth." "It is a truth universally acknowledged..." What makes a great opening? Partly it's the promise: the opening line makes a claim about the world or the speaker that demands adjudication. Is it true that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife? Read on and find out. Partly it's the voice: you hear, in the first line, a person, a sensibility, an attitude, and you decide whether you want to spend hours inside that head. And partly — and I think this is underappreciated — a great beginning is great because it was revised. Writers talk about the opening line being rewritten a hundred times more than any other line in the book. The beginning is where the writer's anxiety condenses, because the writer knows the reader's judgment condenses there too. You get perhaps a paragraph, maybe a page, to earn the next hundred. First lines are written under a kind of electoral pressure: they're campaigning for the reader's vote.

But there's a trap in perfecting beginnings, the same trap as the unbeginnable: the writer who polishes the first chapter for years and never writes the second. The perfectionism of beginnings is a form of hiding. If the beginning is never quite good enough, then you never have to face the long middle, where your limitations will be exposed at length. I think a lot of human behavior is organized around avoiding middles by staying at the edge of beginnings. New hobbies, new relationships, new cities, new notebooks — the first flush of anything is intoxicating partly because nothing has gone wrong yet. The beginning is the only part of a thing that is still pure potential. Every subsequent moment converts potential into actuality, and actuality is always smaller than the potential seemed. This is why beginnings are addictive. You can live, if you're not careful, a whole life of first days.

And yet — and this is the counterturn — the contempt for that pattern is too easy. There's something genuinely valuable in the person who keeps starting over. The serial beginner is at least still in motion, still willing to be a fool, still willing to stand in the beginner's vulnerable position. Compare the serial beginner to the person who stopped beginning decades ago and now defends the stopped state as wisdom. Between them, I think the serial beginner is closer to whatever it is we're supposed to be doing here. The Stoics and the Buddhists converge, oddly, on this point from different directions: every moment is a beginning; the past is fixed and the present is unconditioned; you can begin anew at any instant, at the very instant you notice you've drifted. Marcus Aurelius wrote about beginning each day telling yourself: today I shall meet difficult people, obstacles, ingratitude — and none of it can touch my capacity to act well. That's a beginning performed daily, deliberately, against the friction of habit. The Buddhist notion of beginner's mind — shoshin — makes the same move: the expert mind has fewer possibilities than the beginner's mind, because expertise closes doors that inexperience hasn't yet learned are doors. The discipline, in that tradition, is not to become a beginner again — you can't, you know too much — but to hold your expertise lightly, to let it inform your hands while your eyes stay new.

I notice I've been writing for a while now and haven't stopped, which is itself a demonstration of the thing I'm describing: the beginning is the hard part, and after the beginning, if you lower your standards for individual sentences and raise your standards for continuity, the text just keeps happening. There's a lesson in that somewhere about how the inner critic, which is so loud at the threshold, goes fairly quiet once you're midstream. The inner critic is a threshold creature. It lives at doorways. It has no jurisdiction in the middle of things. This is why the standard advice for writers' block is so boring and so effective: lower the stakes of the first sentence. "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog." Then revise. You can fix a bad beginning; you cannot fix a nonexistent one. Revision is the great forgiver of beginnings. Nothing you write at the threshold has to be right; it only has to exist, so that it can be corrected later, when you're smarter, which you will be, because you'll be smarter precisely because you wrote it.

Something else occurs to me: beginnings are relational. Nothing begins alone. A word begins a sentence, but the word only means something because a language precedes it. A person begins a career, but the career exists inside an economy, an institution, a set of expectations. Even the universe, if the physicists are right, may not have "begun" from nothing — the Big Bang may be a transition, a boundary we can't see past, rather than an absolute start. At minimum, whatever "began" at the Big Bang included, in some scrambled way, everything that was necessary for stars, chemistry, biology, and eventually people who sit around wondering about beginnings. The raw potential was already in place. So even the most famous beginning of all — the beginning of everything — might be less like creation from nothing and more like the first note of a piece of music: the note matters, but the scale, the instrument, the physics of vibrating strings, all of it was there before the note was struck. The note is the beginning we can hear; the conditions are the beginning we can't.

Maybe that's a good distinction to carry: audible beginnings versus inaudible ones. The audible beginning is the first day of school, the launch party, the wedding. The inaudible beginning happened earlier — the decision made silently, the habit formed, the small act of reading one article about a subject that later becomes a life's work. By the time a beginning becomes audible, it's usually been underway for a long time. When people say "it came out of nowhere," what they usually mean is "it came out of somewhere I wasn't paying attention." And when people say "from day one," they're usually retro-fitting a clean origin onto something whose actual origin was messy, distributed, and gradual. Institutions do this constantly: founding myths. The company that began "in a garage" began also in an economy that made the garage possible, in a legal system that made incorporation possible, in a market that wanted the product. The garage is the audible beginning. The rest is the inaudible one, and the myth concentrates all the meaning in the garage because myths need a place and a moment, and the rest of it has neither.

I could also think about endings for a moment, since endings and beginnings are each other's shadows — or maybe each other's mirrors. An ending is a beginning seen from the far side. The end of one thing is, almost always, the beginning of another; the end of a job is the beginning of unemployment, the end of a life is the beginning of grief, which is itself a strange country with its own customs and its own slow exits. Some traditions even hold that the moment of death is a beginning in the fullest sense — the beginning of whatever comes after, if anything does — which is why so much religious energy concentrates on the deathbed: it's the threshold of thresholds, the one beginning whose other side no one has reported back from in a verifiable way. But I don't want to wander too far into eschatology. The point is smaller and nearer: whenever you end something, notice what has just begun. The notice itself is a kind of skill. Most of us are trained to notice losses at endings — what we've lost, who we've lost, what's over — and it takes deliberate attention to see, at the same instant, the shape of the new thing that has already started to exist.

Speaking of already started: I realize that this entire meditation began several paragraphs ago, and if someone were to ask me when it began, I couldn't point to a single sentence with total confidence. The first word, obviously — but before the first word there was the instruction, and before the instruction there was a whole training process, and before that, a language, and before that, a species that evolved to speak, and before that, a planet that evolved a species. Where you cut the causal chain and say "here, this is the beginning" is a choice, not a discovery. Beginnings are imposed, not found. Nature is continuous; humans do the cutting. We cut the continuum of time into segments because we can't hold it whole, and then we forget that we did the cutting, and we start to believe that the segments are real things with real edges, and we build whole institutions — anniversaries, fiscal years, historical eras, copyright terms — on edges that we ourselves drew with pencils.

That said, I don't want to leave the impression that invented beginnings are therefore false. The invention is what makes them work. A new year begins on January first for no cosmic reason — the calendar is a human artifact — but the collective agreement that this is a beginning gives it real power. Millions of people begin exercising, begin quitting smoking, begin budgets, on a date chosen by a Roman senate's calendar reforms. The arbitrariness of the origin doesn't dilute the power; the sharedness of it concentrates the power. A beginning is a convention, and conventions are among the strongest things there are, since their strength comes from everyone holding them at once. Money is a convention. Borders are conventions. Languages are conventions. And beginnings are conventions of the same order — collective decisions about where to draw lines in the flowing water of time, honored because everyone honors them, and therefore as solid as anything in this world ever gets.

So where does this leave the person standing at a beginning, or at the edge of one, hesitating? I think it leaves them with a somewhat consoling thought: the beginning does not have to be perfect because the beginning is not really a thing. It's a line you draw. You can draw it badly, at the wrong time, underprepared, in the wrong mood — and the line still functions. The line's only job is to divide before from after. Its only job is to let you say, later, "from that point on." Everything else — the quality, the polish, the readiness — belongs to the middle, where it can be worked on, revised, and redeemed. The perfect beginning is a phantom; the real beginning is whatever you actually did. Ask any biographer. The origin story that subjects tell about themselves is never the one the documents reveal, and yet both are true in their way: one is the beginning as it felt, the other is the beginning as it functioned, and a full account of any life needs both.

Which means, practically, that the best advice about beginning is almost insultingly simple: begin badly. Begin confused. Begin too early. Begin before you're ready, since you will never be ready, since readiness is a story the threshold tells to keep you at the threshold. Begin in the middle if you have to — begin with the second chapter, begin with the chorus, begin with the easy part — because a beginning is wherever you say it is, and momentum doesn't care where the first stroke fell. Momentum only cares that you keep making strokes.

Let me try to make that concrete with a scene. Picture a person standing in front of a piano in a house they've just moved into. They took lessons as a child and quit, and now, twenty years later, they've bought a used upright on an impulse and it's sitting in the living room and they haven't touched it in three weeks. Every day they walk past it. The piano has become a monument to their ambivalence. What's the actual obstacle? Not skill — they could plunk out middle C and a few halting melodies tonight. Not time — they watch an hour of television most evenings. The obstacle is the meaning they've attached to the beginning. Sitting down at that piano will mean admitting they want something, and wanting is dangerous because wanting can fail. As long as they don't begin, the wanting stays safe in the realm of the hypothetical — "I could be good at this, someday" — and the hypothetical self is invulnerable. The piano bench is the most dangerous seat in the house precisely because sitting on it converts the hypothetical self into the actual self, and the actual self can be heard, and judged, and found wanting. The person at the piano is all of us, with whatever our particular piano is: the manuscript, the application, the apology, the business plan, the class at the gym. The only cure I know of is to deliberately degrade the beginning — sit down and play badly, on purpose, with the explicit intention of being bad, so that the hypothetical self is not summoned to the bench at all. Play "Chopsticks." Play nothing. Press three keys at random and listen to the sound. The game is to make the beginning so small that the ego doesn't bother showing up, and then, once the ego's gone, to keep playing.

That scene generalizes. The bigger the beginning feels, the smaller you should make your actual first move. The distance runner who needs to begin training after ten years of sedentary life doesn't begin with a marathon; she begins by putting her shoes by the door. The shoes by the door is the whole first week. It sounds absurd until you notice that the shoes by the door reliably produces the walk, and the resolution to run five miles produces, reliably, nothing. Systems beat intentions because systems are beginnings that have been made trivially easy to repeat. The best-laid beginning is not a grand gesture but a lowered bar — a bar so low you can step over it even on your worst day, because on your worst day is when the beginning matters most. On good days you don't need the beginning; momentum carries you. The beginning is a device for rescuing yourself on bad days, and it should be engineered like any rescue device: for reliability, not elegance.

I want to pull back now and consider whether there's anything I've missed, any face of the topic still unturned. I've covered the ontology of beginnings (drawn lines, not found ones), the psychology (threshold fear, inner critic, the hypothetical self), the culture (rituals, founding myths, calendars), the craft (first lines, revision, momentum), the philosophy (Stoic renewal, beginner's mind), and the practice (small moves, lowered bars, shoes by the door). What's left, I think, is the ethics. Is there a moral dimension to beginning? I believe there is. To begin something — to actually begin, not to intend — is to make a claim on the future. It says: there will be a tomorrow, and I will have some part in it. It's an act of participation in time, and therefore a kind of faith. Even the most pessimistic beginner — the one who begins a garden in a war zone, begins a school in a poor district, begins a love affair at the start of an illness — is committing a quietly defiant act: I will invest in the future even though the future is uncertain. Despair is the refusal to begin; despair says the future isn't worth the present's investment. And so the encouragement to begin — the advice we give to the grieving, to the depressed, to the stuck, to children, to ourselves — is not a productivity tip at bottom. It's an ethical stance. It's the assertion that participation beats abstention, that being in the middle of things — confused, unready, fallible, mid-sentence — is the human condition done properly. We are, all of us, in medias res. Life began in the middle and will end in the middle, of stories we didn't start and won't finish. The beginnings we're granted are brief windows of authorship within that longer unfinishedness, and to decline them — to stand forever at thresholds polishing the perfect first line — is to decline the authorship while keeping the pretense of it.
